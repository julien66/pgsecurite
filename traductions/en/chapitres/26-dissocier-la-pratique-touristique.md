## Separating tourist flying

A controversial chapter, but an indispensable one. We need to get out of an ambiguity that runs through our whole activity: we sell the same thing to two groups who have nothing in common.

My proposal fits in one sentence: separate tourist “teaching” and tourist instructors from the real ongoing training of pilots in the activity. Those who really take it up need a long-term mentor, and a real one.

### Two activities under one name

On one side, there is the independent pilot. They chose free flight, they trained, they gradually accepted the share of risk and peril that is the essence of the activity. They are the one living the ultimate freedom and the ordeal we have discussed. It is their hero’s journey.

On the other, there is the tandem passenger or the trainee on a first flight or an introductory course. The vast majority will not come back (see “The power of our beliefs”). They come for a sensation, a memory, a nice photo. FFVL members report about 130 tandem accidents every year, and half of all reported accidents happen in supervised flying (see “Who counts our dead?”). They did not choose the ordeal and often have no idea it exists. They were told: “With proper supervision, there’s no risk.”

These two groups fall under two opposing safety logics. The first can claim the right to take risks for themselves. The second has entrusted their life to a professional and rightly expects a level of safety close to that of passenger transport.

### The current paradox

Today we do the opposite of what this distinction would require. We apply the discourse of autonomy to tourism (“paragliding is safe if you respect a framework”), and we let the independent pilot believe that this same discourse protects them. The first is under-informed, the second over-reassured. That is what I called earlier a form of deception: we guarantee an aircraft that is simple, accessible and with controllable risk, when it is not.

### Experts absorbed by tourism

This mix has a direct consequence on pilot training. Our best instructors are now busy managing tourists. Tandem flying is obviously more lucrative, and school training is also designed to capture and manage flows of trainees who come and go with the seasons. The business model is one of discovery, not of progression.

Make no mistake: this is not a text against professionals. Our figures even say the opposite. Accidents in supervised flying are markedly less serious: about one in five leads to serious injury or death, against nearly one in two in unsupervised flying (see “Who counts our dead?”). Our most severe accidents happen exactly where instructors are no longer present: after leaving school, and in the types of flying considered independent.

Apart from a few very personal, school-based or strongly club-backed initiatives, permanent schools where a pilot can really train over the long term, with longitudinal follow-up of their training, are almost non-existent. When any instructor can deliver training around the same introductory or improvement courses, expert instructors become indistinguishable from beginners. For the student, nothing distinguishes the one with twenty years of cross-country and seasoned flying (competition or not) from the one who qualified two years ago.

The real training courses, those that help an already independent pilot progress, are underpaid relative to the responsibilities involved, and therefore under-invested by our experts, who often fly tandems instead. The most precious knowledge in our community, that of pilots who have seen the limits up close, is thus spent taking passengers for rides rather than being passed on. And even when it is passed on, it is not done optimally, for lack of a suitable framework. The types of flying described as independent are hard to teach as things stand (cross-country course, brown level, SIV, etc.), because instructors and pilots are asked to enter a fleeting teaching relationship: a week at most, repeated each year at best, whereas this is precisely when detailed knowledge of each pilot and teacher matters most. The instructor discovers a student they may never see again; the student leaves alone with what they have understood and handles their own follow-up. It is to address this gap that I propose, further on, the figure of the mentor: not to replace the instructor, but to provide the continuity that a one-week course cannot offer.

### The profile and the why

Separating tourism from pilot training requires real work on profiles and on the “why”. Why does this person come to fly? For a sensation, a memory, a holiday outing? To discover an activity they have always dreamed of? Or to really embark on a career as an independent pilot? These motivations are all legitimate, but they call for neither the same content, nor the same teaching, nor the same discourse (see “The hero’s journey” on the variety of what we come looking for in the air).

We must also dare to say it: not every type of flying is necessarily meant for everyone. This is not elitism. It is recognising that mountain cross-country, para-alpinism or acro require a commitment, a relationship with risk and an investment that not everyone wants, or is able, to make. Saying so early is respecting people.

I therefore propose clearly separating three things that are blurred today:

- **The discovery course.** You come to get a taste of flying, closely supervised, with no commitment. It is an experience, not training.
- **The introductory course.** You learn the basic skills and understand what the activity is, including its risks. This is the time to ask yourself, with an instructor, whether you want to go further and why.
- **The first real step in a career as an independent pilot.** This one is a commitment. It opens a long pathway, followed by a mentor, with its own requirements.

The teaching, the discourse and the rigour of these three moments are hard to reconcile. You do not talk in the same way to a holidaymaker who wants a nice, friendly week in the spirit of an outdoor holiday centre and to a future pilot who will one day have to decide alone whether to launch. Mixing the two means either frightening the first or wrongly reassuring the second.

### What separate tourist flying could look like

Separating does not mean banning or condemning professionals, who keep our activity alive. It means recognising that commercial passenger flying is a different activity, which deserves a different framework:

- **Passenger transport standards.** Wider weather margins than for a solo pilot, sites and fields chosen and equipped for this use, equipment checked on a strict schedule.
- **Its own lessons-learned system.** Commercial tandem accidents should be recorded and analysed separately, and shared among professionals according to the principles of just culture.
- **Honest information.** The passenger has the right to know that they are taking part in an aerial activity with a real risk. Saying so will not drive customers away, and it will protect professionals on the day an accident happens.
- **Training that is not sold as an outdoor leisure activity.** A trainee who wants to become a pilot must hear from the start what they are about to do and what being independent means. Discovery and introductory courses can stay on the tourist side. A pilot’s continued training, on the other hand, belongs to a different track, with its own instructors and long-term follow-up.

### Supporting the hero’s journey

If the trainee pilot should no longer be treated as a tourist, what should we offer them instead? For those who really take it up, a long-term mentor, and a real one. I believe we can make the hero’s journey our own. In Campbell’s story, the hero never really sets out alone: someone calls them to adventure, and someone else guides them as they cross the threshold. Our supervised flying could offer the same to every trainee.

**A sponsor.** The sponsor seems to me able to remain optional, but they are the person who takes on the heavy responsibility of the call: the one who brings the trainee into free flight and its community. I imagine a volunteer relationship, of protector and guide. A relationship that matters. The sponsor is a first source of support, someone with whom one can talk about one’s progress, one’s doubts and the customs the community passes on by word of mouth. Agreeing to sponsor someone means recognising that you do not lightly bring a friend into an activity where life is at stake.

**A mentor.** The mentor is the catalyst of skills. They are in charge of training the young padawans of an area. They coordinate the trainee’s pathway from start to finish. Today, training is often fragmented: a course at one school, another elsewhere, a wing bought on a friend’s advice, a progression no one sees as a whole. What is missing is Gandalf or Master Yoda. Someone who knows the hero well and follows their student scrupulously on their journey. Together, they discuss the quest, the reasons for the journey and the best ways to reach the goals: the courses to take, including at other schools, changes of wing, “consumer moments” and the stages of progression. All this must be built with a mentor because the traps are too many and too hard to handle alone.

This mentor is not the instructor or tandem pilot one may often come across. They are a grand master, an undisputed expert in the discipline, recognised as such by all. They have a great deal of flying experience, over a decade of flying in extremely demanding settings such as competition: cross-country, acro, racing. They have seen the limits up close, in others and in themselves. That is precisely what allows them to help a trainee choose their quest without picking the wrong trial.

For me, this relationship between the pilot and their mentor does not end. A pilot may well want to find another mentor along the way, but in the end it does not matter: what counts is that there is always someone. I have been flying at top level for more than a decade, I am a respected coach and a recognised pilot, and yet from time to time I have questions about my flying and need to talk to someone about it. I fly barely more than 100 hours a year, I fly an Enzo 3, always in competition, in varied conditions, trying to follow pilots who are three times my level. I do it above all for professional and social reasons. It is not the best cocktail. How long can it last? Personally, I am lucky to have solid contacts in this world, people who know me, know how to talk to me and look out for me. What happens when you do not have that expertise at hand? What happens when the quality of the advice you get is poor? When it is influenced by a dealer, an organiser, a good friend who is a little too enthusiastic?

Advice in our world is rarely neutral. The person selling the wing has an interest in you changing it, the person organising the event has an interest in you coming, and the friend who flies hard wants you to follow. No one is dishonest for all that, but no one is disinterested. That is precisely one of the norms Merton set for any community seeking the truth (see “Listening to uncomfortable voices”). The mentor must be its guarantee: someone who knows the pilot over time and has nothing to sell them.

We need to build such a system. Help competent professionals to reinvest in long-term training, in their immediate flying area, around goals set differently. Move away from tandem flying, while genuinely finding an economic interest in teaching within real free-flight schools, not in for-profit organisations but where the pilots are, that is, within clubs. Regular supervised flying, detached from seasonality and tourism, and mentoring work should be encouraged and heavily subsidised. Not only must we be able to support every cohort of first-year members, but more broadly almost all our pilots should be followed longitudinally by a professional expert in the discipline. I am not proposing to make this relationship artificial or to impose it, but we could start offering this kind of support to our new pilots and see where it takes us over time. Funding follows the same model as everywhere: the pilot keeps paying their teacher, the club pools resources, the federation subsidises what it can… In case it was not clear, this is about reorienting our leisure clubs towards “sport”. By sport I do not mean competition, but the model is indeed similar to the one a coach has with their athletes. So let us finally (!!) open schools offering regular year-round flying, rather than scattering the same money over crowds of itinerant and/or tourist courses. We can reverse this trend and make what is the rule today become the exception tomorrow, and vice versa. It is fascinating to see that free-flight clubs all organise “club trips” to a host of destinations but do not so often organise flying at home, or at least not on a recurring or professional basis. Even big clubs, with more than 500 members, employ no coach. Our best instructors remain self-employed and work seasonally. The day we manage to change this through the system, without worsening their working conditions, we will instantly gain in safety. It is not impossible, and it is in this kind of challenge that we expect federations and the major players of free flight to step up. In any case, that is all I wish for the world of tomorrow and for the job of future teachers.
