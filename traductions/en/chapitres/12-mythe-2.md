## Myth \#2

Accidents are caused by human error.[^c-mythe2-ex]

### Hindsight bias

As long as the equipment is not directly at fault (e.g. the wing does not break), it will always be possible, after the fact, to rationalise the mistakes made by someone who has had an accident. That is in fact what we spontaneously do in our formal or informal “debriefings”. In the end, with or without them, it will always be the victim’s fault. Their technique, their analysis, their commitment, their choice of wing, their fatigue, their stress, their positioning and above all their decisions… If they ended up on the ground a little too hard, they must have missed something at some point, right?!
Our method is a perfect example of hindsight bias, as widely studied in the scientific literature since the 1970s, notably by Daniel Kahneman[^9] (and colleagues). Once the outcome is known, with plenty of time and all the available information, an outside observer will always be able to rationalise the causes of an accident after the fact and attribute them to human error. It is precisely this method that allows the system to keep faith in its own beliefs, and in particular in the idea that paragliding is a safe activity whose risks can be controlled.

### Victims as scapegoats

I see the community sinking into a very negative self-protective reflex. Those who have accidents are our black sheep. A victim is someone who did something stupid, even, suspiciously, a “sub-par pilot”, a hothead or a reckless fool. The lack of empathy is glaring, the judgement instant. The direct consequence is that victims are ashamed. Most of those who have an accident completely dress up their story, play down what happened to them or do not talk about it. Some even hide and pretend nothing happened. Accidents whose consequences are not extremely serious, immediate or public knowledge are rarely reported. There was a time when even using one’s reserve parachute was viewed with suspicion! Fortunately, its widespread use in acro paragliding and the slogan “One turn, throw the reserve” now help encourage pilots to use their reserve and to own that choice with less embarrassment.

If myth acts as a unifier, a symbolic communion around beliefs and values shared by a group of individuals, anthropologists have also long known that crises threatening unity are resolved by designating a scapegoat. The scapegoat represents the root of the problem that threatens collective reasoning. Ethnologically, this is what helps us understand how human sacrifice was organised. Slaughtering a scapegoat, preferably collectively or as a ritual with an official representative, a notable of the community, restores order, logic and peace, and relieves everyone of guilt.

- “It hasn’t rained for a long time, but the Rain God hasn’t abandoned us. It’s just that idiot of a scapegoat who angered him… We’ve dealt with him, everything will go back to normal now.” (And everyone goes on peacefully believing in the Rain God.)

Many accidents shake the myth of “safe” paragliding that our community maintains. Our victims are therefore treated as scapegoats. We symbolically sacrifice them on the altar of our main belief. We also hold public lynchings from time to time. Not with the same violence or the same fervour as in the past, but you only need to read the comments under accident videos on social media to be convinced. Everyone seems to turn into at least some kind of condescending expert… Often the author of the video apologises profusely and explains their gross error by way of introduction.

This is the price at which the myth of a safe activity with controllable risk is preserved, because, logically, the victim is judged to be at fault for not having controlled their flight. The reasoning is extremely comfortable, since it immediately allows us as pilots to distance ourselves from the accident and to go on thinking that all will be well, since potentially “I wouldn’t have made that mistake, so I wouldn’t have had an accident”. And indeed, most of our community thinks that “the risks of the activity can be controlled, but accidents are caused by pilot error”.

This also explains paraglider pilots’ lack of empathy towards fallen pilots. Despite a heavy and widespread accident record all over the world, there is no specialised association to support victims: “She made a mistake.” “I told him so.” “It was bound to happen.” Online fundraisers to search for or find missing members of our community usually fill up in a few hours, whereas this is no longer automatically the case when it comes to helping the family of a pilot who is in fact found with multiple injuries or dead. The same person can see their status change from someone who is lost and needs help to someone who did something stupid.

### Another perspective

The logical trap is obvious, because in reality no victim wanted their accident. Each pilot surely did their best to avoid it. If there is one interesting feature of human intelligence, it is precisely its propensity to make mistakes. “Errare humanum est […]”. “To err is human” should also be heard more specifically as “I am human, therefore I make mistakes”. Our most careful pilots make mistakes. Even our greatest champions get things wrong, and occasionally they fall because of a blunder. Accidents affect everyone. This does not at all mean we should be fatalistic, still less accept them, and I will come back to this idea. However, in this context, does the “controllable” nature of risk in paragliding not appear particularly illusory? What if those who fell today had simply been hit by statistics and/or bad luck (!) a little earlier than those who will fall tomorrow?

In a paradigm that is less cognitivist and more based on dynamic systems theory or on situated action, it is understood that the error or series of errors leading to the accident is already part of the symptom; that is, a consequence with a deeper, more systemic root. The error cannot be separated from the accident; it is part of it. If we are looking for explanatory mechanisms in order to reduce accidents, we absolutely must move up a level of abstraction and widen the focus around errors, to try to understand not what they are but the conditions in which they arise!

Accidents are not caused by human errors.
They are, at most, human errors that went wrong.

To say “error” is already to judge. The word assumes a norm known in advance: a correct action the pilot should have produced and did not. Yet most of the time we only know that norm afterwards, once the outcome is known. At the time, the pilot took the decision that seemed sensible to them, with the information they had, the fatigue they felt and the pressure of the group or of the day. Sidney Dekker invites us precisely to look for why the pilot’s action made sense to them at the moment they took it[^c-dekker-fg].

What we call an “error” is often an ordinary adaptation. It works a hundred times and, on the hundred-and-first, it meets an unfavourable configuration: a more violent thermal, a wind gradient, another pilot in the wrong place. Erik Hollnagel describes these constant trade-offs between efficiency and thoroughness that each of us makes without thinking[^c-hollnagel]. The same decision makes us a good pilot one day and a “reckless fool” the next, depending on the outcome.

Besides, many errors do not lead to an accident. Some are extremely gross, and we think that this time the pilot really had a narrow escape! We all have in mind some pilot to whom something always happens, for nearly 30 years now, and who has never really suffered any consequences. All this contributes to the illusion of a forgiving sport. Errors, gross or repeated, do not necessarily lead to an accident.

At the same time, we see victims botch a take-off for the very first time and fall to their death. What turns an error into a fatal accident is the fact that the situation goes wrong. Sometimes this can be put down to chance, to the nature of things; sometimes not.

And then there are even cases with no error at all!
Moments when we suffer other people’s errors, equipment-related accidents (yes, really!), all kinds of system faults: a dangerous cable reported nowhere, a race not stopped that degenerates under a thunderstorm.

In any case, there is a view in which accidents are not only caused by human errors. Human errors happen all the time and are rather constants of our human system. They are present at every level of experience and practice, and will happen whether we like it or not, even with all the self-knowledge and all the prevention in the world.

This statement will no doubt sound “fatalistic” to the supporters of the current paradigm. “So there’s nothing we can do?” This misunderstanding stems precisely from their inability to grasp the possibility of action offered by the system, in addition to the human. For an identified category of error, a collective protection system can prevent it from arising in the first place. And even downstream, that is after it has occurred, it is still possible to make flying more robust, so that a blunder that has actually been made does not degenerate into horrific circumstances.

Airline aviation drew the consequences of this observation with *Threat and Error Management* (TEM), developed in the late 1990s by Robert Helmreich’s team at the University of Texas, then adopted by the International Civil Aviation Organization[^c-tem]. The principle is simple. Threats (weather, terrain, traffic, fatigue, equipment…) and errors are considered normal and inevitable. The aim is no longer to eliminate them but to detect them early, manage them, and prevent them from leading to an “undesired aircraft state”, and then to an accident.

Applied to paragliding: a gust on launch is a threat, a poorly controlled inflation an error, a wing veering off to one side just above the slope an undesired state. At each stage, protections are possible. Some are individual, but many are collective: a clear launch area, a well-placed windsock, a pilot helping out, a rule of use for the site. That is the perspective we lack.

### Two conceptions of the accident

We can therefore distinguish two systems that try to characterise what a paragliding accident fundamentally is. These two analytical frameworks are very different and have radical consequences for the preferred remedies. I want to try to summarise the two approaches to illustrate them better. Strictly speaking, they are not mutually exclusive, but the difference in underlying paradigm is obvious and causes significant communication problems within our community.

1\) Paragliding is safe and controllable…
⇔
2\) …but accidents are caused by human error

In this case, we naturally build up a catalogue of errors and accidents:

- “How did the pilot do it?” “What did they do?”
- “What did they miss?”
- “What were they lacking?”
- “What should they have done?”

This essentially cognitivist view holds the individual and their information processing responsible for their motor actions and the associated response. Here, the accident is a gap between a demand imposed by the environment and the insufficient or faulty output of a pilot, itself due to the cognitive processing they carry out when faced with the task. The accident is therefore “caused by human error”.

This line of thinking focuses on the ways of acting and the resources available to the pilot. What could the individual have improved?

Pilot training (!) will almost systematically be put forward as the obvious means of reducing accidents. If accidents are caused by human error, the natural temptation is to reduce pilots’ errors so that they suffer fewer accidents. And this is exactly how pilots, their instructors and their trainers become the main actors of safety, since they are at the heart of training.

The study of human factors becomes essential for prevention and for strengthening each pilot’s self-knowledge. Better-trained pilots who are aware of the pitfalls of human functioning will be less likely to make mistakes and therefore to have an accident.

1\) In paragliding, the pilot’s safety is at stake in the event of an error […]

⇔
2\) Humans make mistakes

Faced with this thorny problem, we might ask questions like these:

- “What happened?”
- “How did the victim get caught out?”
- “What can be done to prevent others from making the same kind of mistake in the future?”

From a dynamic perspective, error and accident are part of the same thing. The situation presents a network of constraints too strong for the individual to adapt to in real time. Their cognitive processing matters less, since here the accident is the product of an error arising (which is always possible) together with its consequences. It is a human error that goes wrong in a given situation.

This line of thinking is open to individual and extra-individual causes. What could have protected the individual?

Errors are a constraint of the human system, a kind of pitfall that cannot be entirely avoided, even through training and self-knowledge. We should rather work to stop the most significant traps from arising for pilots. Group together families of errors that share the same class of problem and invent a collective protection, so that pilots do not have to worry about finding themselves in that situation.

The study of human factors is above all an opportunity to understand how individuals function in order to build future collective/systemic protections for pilots. Training and prevention are often not enough to protect individuals from falling into the traps of their own functioning.

| Paradigm | Flying is safe and controllable […] ⇔ Accidents are caused by human error | The pilot’s safety is at stake in the event of an error […] ⇔ Humans make mistakes |
| :---------- | :------------ | :------------ |
| Accident | Caused by human error | Human error that went wrong |
| Questions | How did the pilot do it? What did they miss? What were they lacking? What should they have done? | How did the victim get caught out? What can be done to prevent this kind of error in the future? |
| Remedy | Individual. Cognitivist. | Collective. Systemic. Complex and multifactorial (situated action). |
| Pilot | To be trained / strengthened. | Imperfect when it comes to error. To be protected. |
| Accident victim | “Made a mistake.” Empathy is difficult. Scapegoat effect. | “Got caught out.” Victim. Compassion. Effect: change the system |
| Human factors | To prevent errors and help the pilot progress | To understand individual weaknesses and improve the system |
| Main actor | The pilot. Instructor. Trainer. | The pilot. Instructor. Trainer. Panel of experts. Community. Institution. Legislator. |

### Implications for safety management

I believe we would gain by changing paradigm and abandoning for good the belief that accidents are caused by human error. That approach rules out looking for external and systemic causes, whereas, conversely, a broader search for the conditions in which pilots’ errors arise does not prevent us from considering either training pathways or human factors.

Changing paradigm does not mean taking responsibility away from pilots. It means changing the question we ask after an accident. Instead of “what did they miss?”, asking “what made this error possible, and what could have limited its consequences?” The first question produces a culprit and a feeling of safety for those left behind. The second produces lines of action: a site to redesign, information to share, a practice to change, equipment to improve, training content to review.

In terms of safety management, this implies three things:

- Collect accidents and incidents without looking for fault, otherwise no one will talk.
- Analyse these events by family rather than one by one, to spot recurring scenarios.
- Share the remedies among all the actors: pilots, instructors, manufacturers, site managers, organisers, federations.

Training keeps its full place, but it stops being our only answer.

### James Reason’s model

In the 1990s, James Reason developed a theory that has become essential. It is well known in our world, and I am sure the diagram below will immediately ring a bell for the reader.
*![](../images/reason-modele-des-plaques.png)*

Reason was one of the first authors to model a risk management method by focusing on what he calls “failures”. He categorised three potential sources:

- Technical failures
- Human errors
- Organisational failures

It was through him that people began to consider “error inseparable from human intelligence”. His conception of safety management consists of stacking protective layers that are known to be individually imperfect, but which together will end up giving the system acceptable reliability. The holes in each of the slices represent this inherent imperfection of each element. These holes are not fixed: for the same pilot, on the same site, they open and close from one day to the next, depending on fatigue, conditions, equipment or the group. Reason shows how it is necessary to add many of them and to play on their complementarity to reduce the likelihood of accidents. This complementarity comes from the diversity of the protections used. If accidents are judged too numerous and the system unreliable, it is because slices are missing or because they do not differ enough from one another; they then probably all have the same holes.

The greatest safety thinkers in our community have successfully taken up this theoretical model to propose a risk management method applied to paragliding. Jean-Marc Galan[^10] is certainly the best known of them, through his reference book and then his aptly named lecture: “La revanche d’Icare” (Icarus’s Revenge).

Yet the Reason model systematically offered to us in its paragliding version still only deals with the human error part. When we look at the sequence of slices presented, we find only protections concerning the individual in their flying: their mental state, their physical condition, their technique, their knowledge of the environment, and so on. In doing so, we are in fact only looking at a third of what Reason recommends. Out go the organisational protections, out go the equipment protections. In its various versions, the best authors in our community all seek to prevent or to train in order to reduce the possible “holes” in pilots’ individual protection systems. This is extremely useful, and we will come back to it, but it is not exactly what Reason proposes in his risk management model. While we try to shrink the holes in each slice, he proposes adding others, organisational and technical, to block the residual failures. We must accept that the slices protecting pilots from their own human errors have and always will have holes. Only on that condition will we look for the other organisational and technical shields to protect this or that type of pilot in this or that context.

This is precisely what we cannot do while we stay in the paradigm that focuses its remedies on pilot training. No pilot will ever be completely “finished” or “out of danger”. Education and prevention never come to an end, and that is why, despite one of the most advanced training systems in the world, with high-quality people, French statistics continue to show an overall risk management system that is not very reliable. We are simply not strengthening the right things.

Can we believe that the thinkers of our community, however brilliant, have difficulty imagining a more comprehensive Reason model that also deals with organisational and technical failures? Not for a second. The reasons why authors do not illustrate their point in this way are mainly of two kinds:

First, training and individual risk management are essential. They are the main and legitimate concern of pilots, and each pilot must indeed take responsibility and become autonomous in the face of the risks inherent in their flying. It is in-depth work for which references were lacking anyway.

Second, we have to mention the dominant paradigm of our community and the deliberately “FREE” culture of paragliding, especially in France and Europe. There is no shortage of ideas for collective safety, but for now it is extremely difficult and unpopular to try to get institutional or organisational layers of safety accepted. They are perceived as restrictions on the flying of community members. There is a real convergence of interests in continuing to talk about accidents as something owed to other people’s mistakes, because it avoids a new set of organisational or technical shields deemed to be a threat to freedom. After all, “we’re not going to punish a whole community because some people are turkeys[^11], are we?”

I want to make clear that, like others, I fell into this trap and this belief when I was younger. I signed a fierce open letter when the federation bodies (the International Hang Gliding and Paragliding Commission and the French Free Flight Federation) banned the use of prototypes and uncertified wings in their competition circuit[^12]. Ironically, eight days before signing that letter, I had myself crashed heavily at the controls of one of those machines.

### Human factors hijacked

We must go further, because even work on human factors has been diverted from its nature. Originally, human factors were a discipline of engineers and ergonomists. Just after the Second World War, Paul Fitts and Richard Jones studied hundreds of “pilot errors” in the US Army Air Forces. Their conclusion remains famous: most of these errors are explained by the design of the controls and instruments, and it is the cockpit that must be corrected, not the pilot[^c-fitts]. The whole discipline was born from this reversal: knowing the limits of humans in order to build them a system that tolerates those limits.

In paragliding, we have kept the words and reversed the meaning. Human factors have become a catalogue of biases and mental traps: goal pressure, overconfidence, group effects, fatigue, selective reading of the weather. This work is valuable for knowing oneself, and it has improved training. But in an accident debriefing, it most often serves only one purpose: to explain what the victim should have seen in themselves[^c-france3]. The pilot is no longer only guilty of their error; they are also guilty of failing to outwit their own biases. The knowledge that was supposed to protect them is now used to condemn them.


This is not a marginal drift: it is the doctrine written in black and white in our reference book. The “Facteur humain et sécurité” (Human factors and safety) chapter of the FFVL’s *Manuel du vol libre*, in its 2005 edition, opens with this “first observation”: “the pilot’s responsibility is almost systematically at issue”. Even collisions are no exception: if one of the two pilots is at fault, “the other pilot, if they keep a lookout on either side of their path, should be able to avoid them”. The “second observation” lists “fatigue, stress and behaviour” among the leading causes, and the text states that “accidents happen most of the time to the pilot whose reactions are inappropriate or absent”[^c-manuel-fh]. A few pages later, human factors become a list of character flaws, of what one must not be on the ground: “in too much of a hurry”, “too sure of oneself, pretentious or a show-off”, “too stubborn”, “too proud”, “too absent-minded”, “too selfish”[^c-manuel-comportements]. Here the human factor is no longer something the system must take into account, but a vice each pilot must correct in themselves.

To be fair, the same chapter also contains, almost by accident, a perfectly systemic lesson. Forgetting to hook in on a hang glider is described as responsible for “about 1 serious accident in 2”, and the text notes that “year after year, since the sport has existed, this has not changed”. For rigging errors, on the other hand, it is not the pilots who have changed: it is the manufacturers, who have made “great efforts to make ‘idiot-proof’ wings”, to the point that with a modern wing it is “almost impossible to make a rigging error”. The manual also recommends that “pilots check each other”[^c-manuel-materiel]. It is all there: when we wanted to eliminate error, it was the system we changed, and it rather worked. That is exactly Fitts and Jones’s lesson. It has simply never been extended to the rest.

This way of seeing things is found well beyond the schools, right up to social media, where coaches and well-known pilots share their advice with great sincerity. This is not a criticism of them: it is our common culture expressing itself through them. A widely followed American pilot and coach illustrates it well[^c-despein]:

> “The key to fly and not dying: the best pilots in the world manage their psychology very intentionally, and the difference between someone who flies a long fulfilling career and someone who crashes and hurts themselves and dies can often be just one momentary lapse in their psychological, physiological or emotional states.”

The advice is good, and every pilot gains from following it. Yet another lesson can be drawn from it: if a life hangs on a single moment of inattention, then our safety system leaves no margin for the human being. A well-designed system would do exactly the opposite. It would accept that these lapses happen, since they will, and make sure they are no longer enough to kill.

Many of his videos analyse accidents starting from the pilot’s choices. He sincerely wants to do good. He is convinced that understanding some people’s mistakes can prevent others from making them, and that is a respectable conviction. He explains it himself with great emotion[^c-despein-2]:

> “I have this kind of crazy belief that in every […] there is like nuggets, there is like seeds […] And it’s my job to like dig into these tragedies and to water them and to water them and water it and grow them into an orchard that feeds people and that makes me better and that makes life mean something.”

The intention is beautiful, and I share it: every accident must serve a purpose. But the method matters as much as the intention. When we look for the seeds only in the pilot’s mistakes, the orchard risks producing a single crop: the conviction that the victim should have acted differently. In my view, these analyses can then be experienced as guilt-inducing by victims and their families, even if that is never their aim. They can also reinforce, in those watching, the idea that it will not happen to them since they will not make the same mistake. Just culture suggests looking for seeds elsewhere too: in the site, the weather, the equipment, the training, the organisation. That is also where lessons are found that protect everyone, including on the day we get it wrong.


Sidney Dekker describes this shift precisely. In what he calls the “old view”, human error is the cause of the accident, and the work consists of finding who got it wrong. In the “new view”, human error is the symptom of a deeper problem in the system, and the work begins where the old view’s investigation stops[^c-dekker-fg]. We are using the tools of the new view in the service of the old one.

Used true to their nature, human factors should lead us to a different question. Since we know that pilots are sensitive to goal pressure, that they misread the weather after a two-hour drive, that they get carried along by the group, what can we change around them so that these traps close less often? That is what helping the pilot systemically means: not asking them to be stronger than their nature, but building a system that takes it into account.

Current accident databases fail precisely on this point. They focus on and collect what they believe to be the essentials: pilots’ errors. The DHV thus presents the “Pilotenfehler” (pilot errors) directly in the summary of each accident analysis, right on the home page of its website. Errors form the backbone of the analysis: the pilot’s hours (recent and total), their wing, their professional and marital situation, their fitness, their last SIV course, what they missed, what they did not see, what they should have done… The information collected is then used to reinforce the analysts’ confirmation bias. Too few analysis reports and accident databases offer a structure capable of collecting systemic data. Too often, the recommendations come down to one conclusion: the pilot, their condition, the associated prevention and their training. That is incomplete.

Before going further into the beliefs and imagination of our community, we should pause on the essential notion of freedom and its systematic confrontation with regulation, standards and collective management systems. Let us try to understand what the concept of free flight really contains and how it relates to safety.
