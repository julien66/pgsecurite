## Myth \#3

Safety is an individual matter. “The only actor ever able to ensure their safety is, and always will be, the pilot.”

### Master on board, under God

The argument of the sacrosanct aircraft commander or ship’s captain has had its day, and no doubt for very good reasons. The expression enshrines the position of responsibility of the person in command. They are granted full powers (or almost!) in order to maximise every chance of bringing the aircraft, crew, passengers and cargo safely home. They can thus adapt, improvise and manage exceptional situations requiring immediate and decisive action.

However responsible in the eyes of the law or of others, the aircraft commander does not have divine powers. Their obligation of result is limited to that of their aircraft manufacturer, their air traffic controller, their airline… The system that makes civil aviation safe is very complex and far-reaching. The pilot is certainly one of its main actors, but it would be naive to think they could be solely responsible for the safety of the flight. Many things are far beyond them, even when they relate directly to their own competence (training, health, etc.). Legislators are not fooled, moreover, and place the obligation of result directly on the airline.

Realistically, the commander’s scope of action can be redefined as being responsible for “the safe conduct of the flight”. That is already a lot! They manage the real situation of the flight, from its preparation to its completion, and do everything possible with their crew to carry out their mission successfully.

### Someone responsible for safety?

No one can be responsible for a complex process that only partly depends on them. That is what safety is: a complex process involving many actors and procedures working together to achieve a safe and reliable result. Paraglider pilots are the only ones who can manage the proper conduct of their flight. Unfortunately, that does not guarantee complete safety. The belief in a safe and controllable activity (myth \#1) makes it easy to transfer responsibility to a single individual, who finds themselves promoted to being responsible for their own safety. It is a tremendous illusion. In reality, the ecosystem that affects safety is complex and involves many actors! Wing designers, the laboratories certifying equipment, training providers, manufacturers, instructors and even the general mindset of the community shape pilots’ safety, without any one of them being able to do much about it individually. In free flight, the product of this interweaving is clearly not sufficient in terms of safety, whereas it is in commercial aviation.

This myth is not just landing-field bar talk. It is written into the very first pages of the FFVL’s *Manuel du vol libre*, in a box entitled “Être pilote” (Being a pilot): “The independent pilot is solely responsible for their decisions to fly, whatever the site and their equipment”[^c-manuel-etre-pilote]. Legally, the statement is understandable. But it says much more than that: it establishes the idea that the site, the equipment and every situation make no difference to the question. Yet aviation and every high-risk industry teach us precisely the opposite.

The federation repeats it in its own prevention materials. A safety awareness slideshow distributed by the FFVL around 2016 concludes: “The first threat to your safety is you! The first remedy is you too!”[^c-ffvl-diaporama] Yet the same document stated a few pages earlier that “incidents and accidents are individual; safety can progress collectively”. It is all there: the accident remains the pilot’s business, and the collective only steps in afterwards, to draw lessons from it.

It is time to understand that we do not manage risk alone, independently, in our own corner. Our whole environment influences that management, and our statistics show that pilots currently need help in the face of risk. I want to turn the wording of this myth completely around: safety is a collective matter. Pilots today are ill-equipped to ensure their own safety… Even when they do not yet know it, and even when they claim the fundamental right to manage it all by themselves.

Industry has a tool for reading this deadlock: the Bradley curve, popularised by the chemical group DuPont in the mid-1990s[^c-bradley]. It describes four stages of maturity of a safety culture. At the *reactive* stage, that of “natural instincts”, safety is everyone’s own business, and accidents an inevitable cost of the activity. At the *dependent* stage, it relies on rules and supervision. At the *independent* stage, everyone makes it a personal value and takes care of themselves. Finally, at the *interdependent* stage, safety becomes collective: we look out for others and accept that they look out for us. Reread the “Being a pilot” box in the light of this curve: the pilot “solely responsible for their decisions” is the very definition of the first stage. Free flight has built demanding training to bring each pilot to the independent stage, but it has never climbed the last step. The curve is not a scientific law: it has hardly been validated by independent studies, and the figures on falling accident rates that accompany it come mainly from DuPont itself. But as a lens, it shows clearly where we stand.
