## Conclusion

Free-flight people dream as much as they defend the reliability and safety of their activities. A dream that is sadly unfinished, more than forty years after the first defensive reflexes needed to support an emerging activity faced with risks and accidents. The five myths we have described are the foundation of the stability of a community concerned about the risk-taking and accidents inherent in its activity. It is a complex belief system, shared by all free-flying pilots, serving at once the balance, the justification and even the evangelisation of paragliding as a discipline.

This belief system has one striking feature: it does not hesitate to divert concepts to justify itself. We have seen this several times. Wilde’s risk homeostasis theory, which describes how participants adjust their behaviour, has become an argument for refusing any external measure. Reason’s slice model, designed to stack technical, human and organisational protections, has kept only the individual’s slices in our world. Human factors, born to adapt the system to the human being, are used to explain what the victim should have seen in themselves. And the collapse, the main failure of our aircraft, has become a guarantee of safety. Each time, the tool is good. It is the way it is used that turns it around. It is probably not intentional: no one got together to twist Wilde or Reason. But it adds up, and all these diversions point in the same direction, the one that protects our beliefs. We take concepts made to change the system, and we use them to change nothing.

Before going further, we must do justice to what has been done. The current, pilot-centred approach has not failed: it has reached its limits. It has built one of the most advanced training systems in the world, with high-quality instructors, pilot ratings, SIV courses and reference books such as those by Pierre-Paul Ménégoz or Jean-Marc Galan. It has improved equipment, to the point that today’s wings have little in common with those of the pioneers (see “Deceptive sturdiness”). It has introduced certification tests, networks of weather stations, and a slogan, “One turn, throw the reserve”, which changed attitudes to the reserve parachute. It has even produced, almost without saying so, genuine systemic protections: “idiot-proof” hang glider wings, self-locking karabiners, devices against forgetting leg straps. And our figures prove it right on one essential point: where instructors are present, accidents are markedly less serious (see “Who counts our dead?”).

This book does not ask us to throw all that away. It asks us to add the missing slices. Individual responsibility remains indispensable; it is simply not enough.

We are reaching the end of a cycle. The growth in the number of pilots, the recent increase in accidents and their shift towards greater impact on supervised flying and experienced pilots no longer make it possible to maintain this status quo and the usual rhetoric of safe paragliding with controllable risk. When it comes to safety, the community is no longer making progress by blaming those who have accidents and by basing almost all its efforts on pilot training, awareness campaigns and the study of human factors.

Democratisation has made its way, and with it the diversification of the ways people fly. It is no longer possible to uphold the ideology of the responsible victim and the scapegoat, because some victims and/or their instructors no longer fit the profile at all, and there are more and more of them anyway. It is noticeable that some victims have broader shoulders than others. Somehow, a high-mountain guide, an instructor, an old pilot or a test pilot makes a much better victim than a young city woman arriving on an introductory course in our world of the air. It is easier to rationalise pilot error, getting stuck in habits, recklessness, poor decision-making, the lure of performance, pride… and conversely, it is harder to accept the death of someone culturally further removed from the activity. That is the direction we are heading in. Many observers see this as yet another consequence of our societies’ excessive focus on individual liability. And it is true that there is an undoubtedly appalling social trend towards wanting to sanitise everything, rationalise everything, insure everything and make everyone liable:

- “You can’t insure the mountains.”
- “American-style lawsuits… soon we won’t be able to do anything.”

While this ideological battle is certainly commendable, that is not what this is about. The problem is that, as a community, we benefit from the spread of a belief system that remains effective for winning converts while producing many accidents. The tension does not lie in placing too much liability on pilots or their instructors. It lies in the ambiguity between what the community tries to do with paragliding and what it boasts about it to others. There is a form of deception, usually unintentional, in free flight today, because we structure the activity mainly around leisure and managing tourist flows, guaranteeing an aircraft that is simple, accessible and with controllable risk, when it is not and never has been. We give participants additional ways to take an active part in the society of the spectacle[^27] without saying a word about what they are actually about to do. The cult sketch by Belgian comedian François Damiens illustrates the whole absurd and powerful dimension of this situation: posing as a paragliding instructor, he wraps his passenger in a thick bundle of lines and asks him to untangle it all later in flight. Absolutely nothing that is asked seems abnormal to the layperson: not the partly torn wing, not the dazed instructor, not the “tweet / tweet-tweet” he is supposed to say… The (good-natured) passenger believes and does what is needed, because it is understood that the activity is safe and that nothing will happen to him. Indeed, that is exactly what he was sold: “With proper supervision, there’s no risk.”

Free flight is not condemned to stay this way. There are deep reasons why humans want to fly. I think it important to amend our beliefs with less pretence and by going back to the source of these aspirations. The freedom of flight hides a powerful symbolism that we have only touched on with the concepts of the hero’s journey and the ordeal. On this ground, risk, peril and death are omnipresent and are part of the quest (which ceases to be a game). There is no certainty, there is no guarantee, and obviously this activity does not sell as well, or at least sells very differently.

Our accident problem will find answers at the exact confluence of the strong democratisation we are witnessing and the completely outdated culture we keep alive. I see two possible directions for the future.

We can stay in the paradigm of “accidents caused by human error”, provided we accept remaining small and probably shrinking slightly. Contrary to a widespread idea, public authorities do not have much of a problem allowing highly risky activities to go ahead. Jumping off a cliff with a parachute, diving from a 30 m bridge… There is no shortage of Red Bull-style ideas, and in reality they are not fought that much. At least not as long as they stay on the margins and concern only a small number of individuals. Our institutions can go on maintaining that victims make mistakes and that the problem lies in pilot training and human factors, as long as the number of injured remains socially acceptable and their profile remains consistent.

“Staying small” means cutting off much of the democratisation of our activities, notably to the detriment of those culturally furthest from flying and risk. For a start, it is not very much in the spirit of Saint-Exupéry; we have talked about the emancipating role of our aircraft. What is more, it would restrict the socio-economic side of our community and the bulk of our activity, that of the professionals. Less consumption, less tourism, fewer newcomers and fewer conversions, refocusing on groups culturally closer to the outdoors, the mountains, risk and flying.

“Growing big”, on the other hand, can no longer go on being based on a series of myths or lies. We must learn to talk about our activities better, to design them and sell them differently. Our prevention, training and human factors policies are failures because we are not looking at the right things, continuing to focus only on the mistakes made by pilots while at the same time glorifying the mind-blowing performances of a handful of others. Our community is overwhelmed by accidents, and we must accept a shift in how we look at ourselves in order to take on collective responsibilities. Change our mindset, overturn our certainties and founding myths in order to better integrate new ways of working and new habits, and move towards something safer and more sustainable. If this last thought should remind you of another similar emergency (the climate), any resemblance would be purely coincidental.

Taking collective responsibility can easily go hand in hand with more regulation, more structure, more bans. It is an almost inevitable pitfall; however, we have seen the specific features of our activities and the complexity of their relationship with the notion of freedom. In free flight, if we want to win support for any measure or change in the direction of safety, we must try to spare the notions of freedom attached to the activity as much as possible.

This dilemma has been very well described by René Amalberti, a physician and specialist in the safety of high-risk systems. With Charles Vincent, he distinguishes three main safety models[^c-amalberti]. The ultra-adaptive model, that of activities that embrace risk, such as Himalayan mountaineering or deep-sea fishing: safety relies on the expertise, autonomy and adaptability of a few remarkable individuals. The high-reliability model, that of teams who manage risk without avoiding it, such as aircraft carriers or firefighters. And the ultra-safe model, that of commercial aviation or nuclear power, which avoids risk through standardisation, supervision and limits on individual autonomy. Amalberti also shows that an activity does not move from one model to another without changing its nature: becoming ultra-safe means accepting to give up part of one’s maximum performance and autonomy[^c-amalberti-2005].

That is exactly our problem. Free flight today is an ultra-adaptive activity that sells itself as if it were ultra-safe. I do not believe we should, or even could, become commercial aviation: that is not paragliding. But between Himalayan mountaineering and air transport, there is room for a free activity that equips itself with collective protections.

I have chosen to try to respect the symbolic dimension that flight holds for the pilot. The ultimate freedom, that of confronting death, must remain, at least symbolically. If we tried to stop our activities from offering an ordeal-like character to those who take part, they would immediately cease to exist as such. It would be something else; we would simply have killed paragliding or hang gliding. In this sense, I do not want to defend a sanitised, “regulationist”, “rigorist” line. We can see elsewhere what distorting outdoor disciplines for the sake of democratisation, commercialisation and safety can do. I hope indoor paragliding never exists, I prefer competitions in the mountains, and at the same time I want to put forward a few proposals and ideas for the future. Some are collective “tips” for the community or institutions; others aim to structure things by meeting pilots on their own ground, within their own symbolism, and simply trying to offer them a better hero’s journey!
