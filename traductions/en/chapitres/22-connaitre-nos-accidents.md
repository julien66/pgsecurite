## Knowing our accidents

Before acting, we need to know what is happening to us: where, how, and according to which scenarios.

### Mapping accidents. Identifying the sites and areas that cause the most accidents

The statistics currently available to us (French and general, mainly for insurance purposes) show the following distribution of accidents: ⅓ on take-off, ⅓ in the air and the last third on landing. The 2024 and 2025 reports refine this picture: landing and approach account for more than 40% of accidents, take-off about a third, and flight one in five to one in four (see “Who counts our dead?”).

Through this mapping work, the take-offs and landing fields that cause the most accidents (there are some!) could be reported to the site managers concerned. I know landing fields with appalling statistics, some of them in sometimes “renowned” places. In some cases there is no other land available, and then the problem becomes thorny. Occasionally, on the contrary, there are alternatives, and we should make the effort to swap or redesign the usual field. Wherever the statistics are bad and the take-off or landing cannot be changed, we can flag a site as highly accident-prone with some form of signage.

There are slopes, gullies and ridges known for markedly disorganised convective activity even in standard flying conditions. In some areas, breezes naturally organise themselves to become stronger than our aircraft can normally handle. We also know more and more about convergences and breeze/wind shifts in certain places, with difficult consequences in the air and especially on approach. The fact is that accidents happen repeatedly in the same places. They often affect the same type of pilot, without us building on the knowledge acquired to prevent them effectively. We zealously map thermals and good flight lines, but we do not flag the areas we know full well to be dangerous.

We can also cross-reference accidents with particular weather or air-mass conditions in a given area. Wind strength in mountainous terrain is known to be a bad catalyst for accidents. Let us prove it and prevent it.

### Reading our most frequent accidents through TEM

Mapping is not enough: we also need to understand how our accidents build up. The TEM method presented earlier (see “Myth #2”) offers a simple framework. For each scenario, we look for the **threats** (what comes from the environment or the equipment and is beyond the pilot’s control), the **errors** (what the pilot does or fails to do), the **undesired states** (the degraded situation preceding the accident) and the **countermeasures** (what makes it possible to detect and correct before it is too late). The point is not to name a culprit but to see where the system can act.

The figures that follow come from the 1,449 accident reports made to the FFVL in 2024 and 2025. They are partial, since not all accidents are reported, but they give an order of magnitude[^c-ffvl-export-tem].

#### In hang gliding: landing, then take-off

Hang gliding accidents are rare in the reports (49 in two years for 350 members), but their distribution is clear: 32 relate to landing, including 13 base bar impacts, and 11 to take-off, including 8 falling back onto the slope.

- **Landing.** *Threats*: wind gradient, terrain turbulence, tail or crosswind, short or sloping field. *Errors*: approach too slow, flare too early or too late, turning close to the ground, misjudging the wind. *Undesired state*: wing below flying speed a few metres off the ground, with no margin to pick up speed. *Countermeasures*: drogue chute, a codified approach with a minimum speed, choosing a prepared field, flare training, wheels on the base bar, a landing field always within glide.
- **Take-off.** *Threats*: crosswind, gusts, shallow slope. *Errors*: insufficient run-up, wing poorly held, launching below flying speed. *Undesired state*: wing not yet flying while the pilot has left the ground. *Countermeasures*: launch site improvements, rigorous pre-flight check and wind reading, accepting to give up, training on a training slope.

#### In paragliding: three scenarios that kill

- **Collapses close to the ground.** This is the leading fatal scenario: of the 23 paragliding deaths reported in 2024 and 2025, 10 were in-flight collapses. Of 148 collapse-related accidents, nearly half end in serious injury or death. And where height is recorded, the first signs appear below 100 metres in more than eight cases out of ten (76 out of 89), where the reserve did not have time to be thrown or to open. This is the “dead man’s zone”. A reserve parachute can physically be operational within 50 metres of height loss, but for that it must be thrown cleanly and in a favourable configuration (sink rate, clearance). What the statistics say is that in this 100 to 150 m zone, the time needed to recognise the situation, fly, decide and act weighs on the chances of success. It goes too fast for many people, including outstanding pilots. We must stop thinking that pilots “just had to throw it” and accept that humans like us generally find it hard to do so in these conditions. *Threats*: thermal or mechanical turbulence close to terrain, strong and gusty breeze, a demanding and sensitive wing. *Errors*: flying too close to terrain in strong conditions, inappropriate piloting, a wing above one’s level. *Undesired state*: collapsed wing with no height to recover. *Countermeasures*: innovation work on the reserve parachute, automatic deployment, keeping a height and distance margin from terrain in proportion to the conditions, bonus points for staying away from terrain in online cross-country contests or competitions, choosing a wing suited to one’s real level, practising throwing the reserve.
- **Water landings.** They are rare but very serious: 11 reports in two years, including 3 deaths. *Threats*: lake or river on the flight path, landing by the water, wind pushing towards the water, ridge lift weakening with no landing option. *Errors*: poor management of the flight plan or approach, underestimating the danger of water. *Undesired state*: pilot in the water, strapped into their harness, under the wing and lines. *Countermeasures*: self-inflating life jacket and an accessible hook knife, knowing how to unclip quickly, specific training, possible boat safety cover.
- **Forgetting to clip in.** Very few are reported (3 in two years, including one death), probably because they are often caught just in time or never told. *Threats*: interruption during preparation, busy launch, fatigue. *Errors*: leg or chest straps not closed, karabiner not properly locked. *Undesired state*: pilot in the air no longer held by their harness. *Countermeasures*: a systematic pre-flight check, always in the same order; harnesses that prevent launching unless clipped in; cross-checking between pilots.


### More precise GPS tracks: towards an IGC2

Our instruments record almost all our flights. These tracks are often the only objective source available to an investigator after an accident. Yet the IGC format, which has become the free-flight standard, was designed for one position per second at best. That is the interval required at world championships, and even the “fast” mode triggered by the pilot (*Pilot Event*) settles for one point per second for 30 seconds. The specification does allow tenths of a second to be added, but only as an option[^c-igc]. And in practice, many devices record even less often: FLARM instruments, for example, are set by default to one point every two to four seconds[^c-flarm]. Technically, our GPS units and variometers can do much better. But one second is far too long to understand an incident or accident in detail. A collapse, a surge, a collision or a fall often happens in less time than that.

I see this every time I analyse tracks for an investigation report. Different recordings often have to be cross-referenced, and even then, file resolution remains the main obstacle to reconstructing the last seconds of the flight.

Without going as far as fitting a mini black box, we could agree, pilots, instrument manufacturers and national or international federations alike, on a higher recording rate and on the common adoption of an “IGC2” format. It could also keep the accelerometer and heading data that some of our instruments already measure. A realistic solution for manufacturers would be to keep the latest flights in ultra-detailed form, at 5 Hz, then thin out older ones to 1 Hz. A kind of rolling black box, needing little memory. And it is precisely the latest flights that we need to analyse in detail. These files could feed directly into the accident database, with the agreement of the pilot or their family.
