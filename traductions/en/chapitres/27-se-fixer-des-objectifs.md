## Setting ourselves targets

Everyone talks about safety: federations, schools, manufacturers, organisers, clubs. But what do we concretely expect from it? Who gives themselves a real project, or even a quantified forward plan? I have seen announcements, campaigns, projects and concrete measures, but we very rarely write down in black and white the intended result, or the date on which we will check it.

Should we aim for zero deaths? This book has shown at length why and how the very nature of our activity seems to run counter to such an ambition.
Should we then define a threshold of acceptability? Per pilot or per hour flown? That seems morally very difficult.

In any case, without a target, a safety policy can neither succeed nor fail. A good year becomes proof that the measures work; a bad year becomes a stroke of fate. All the proposals this book has put forward are only worth anything if, after a few years, we can say whether or not they have helped. I would like to propose at least two fundamental criteria for this.

### First criterion: breaking the mechanical rise

The first is almost arithmetical. More pilots means more accidents. If the risk stays the same for each pilot, the number of accidents and deaths simply follows the number of pilots.

And that number is rising. The FFVL went from 34,387 memberships in 2023 to 37,680 in 2026, more than 4% a year over the last two years (see “The power of our beliefs”)[^c-obj-licences]. Since 2007, the federation has lost on average about 5 paraglider pilots per 10,000 memberships each year: 4.4 between 2007 and 2016, 5.9 between 2017 and 2021, 5.0 between 2022 and 2025[^c-obj-tendance]. At this rate, 37,680 memberships give about 19 deaths a year. With twice as many members, which our current growth makes conceivable in less than twenty years, it would be nearly 40. Without anyone having flown any worse.

The first target is therefore to stop this mechanical increase: for the number of serious accidents and deaths to stop following the number of pilots. It is not an ambitious target. It is the minimum. But it is a first sign that safety measures and policies are paying off. With growth of 4% a year, keeping the same number of accidents already requires each pilot’s risk to fall by about 4% a year, that is, by a third in ten years[^c-obj-calcul].

This criterion must be read over several years, and not only on deaths. We have seen (see “Who counts our dead?”) that with a few dozen deaths a year, a difference of three or four deaths proves nothing. The roughly 700 annual accident reports give a more solid measure. Between 2024 and 2025 they remained stable, 728 then 721, while the number of memberships rose by about 4%[^c-obj-declarations]. That is encouraging, but two years do not make a trend. And the trend in deaths over 2017–2025, a fall of about 5% a year, is not statistically significant[^c-obj-tendance]. So we do not yet know whether we have reached this first milestone. Setting the target is giving ourselves the means to find out.

### Second criterion: no longer seeing the same accidents

The second criterion is more demanding. It concerns the repetition of accidents by type.

Seeing an accident happen again really is an admission of failure for safety projects. A first accident can come as a surprise. The second, in the same scenario, much less so: we knew. And our accidents do repeat themselves. Collapses close to the ground account for 10 of the 23 paragliding deaths reported in 2024 and 2025 (see “Knowing our accidents”). In 2026, eight more fatal accidents happened on cross-country flights after collapses close to the ground, with no reserve deployed (see “Who counts our dead?”). The same take-offs, the same landing fields, the same gullies come up in the reports year after year.

The target then consists of following each major family of accidents, those that a TEM reading makes it possible to describe, and setting a result to be achieved for each. Collapses close to the ground, water landings, failing to clip in, hang glider landings: for each scenario, how many accidents today, how many targeted in five years. And every time an accident happens again in an already known scenario, the question to ask is no longer just “what did the pilot do?” but “what have we changed since the last one?”. If the answer is “nothing”, the failure is first and foremost that of the system.

This criterion has one advantage: it forces us to link each measure to a specific scenario. A reserve parachute campaign is judged on collapses close to the ground. Life jackets are judged on water landings. Marking a landing field is judged on accidents at that field. This is how the mapping, the database and the lessons-learned system proposed above take on their full meaning: they do not just serve to understand, they serve to check.
