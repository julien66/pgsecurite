# Who counts our dead?

Who today can say how many pilots have lost their lives paragliding? No one. Not the French Free Flight Federation, not the International Hang Gliding and Paragliding Commission, not the European Hang Gliding and Paragliding Union (EHPU), not a single manufacturer, insurer or researcher. After nearly forty years of flying, our community does not know how to count its dead. This is not a technical detail. It is, I believe, the first symptom of everything this book tries to describe.

### Rick Masters, the first to count

The first to really try was not an institution. It was one man, and a controversial one. Rick Masters is a veteran American hang glider pilot. For years he has been recording paragliding deaths around the world from press reports, and he publishes his figures on his websites and his blog[^c-masters-blog].

His reasons are not mine. Rick Masters is convinced that the paraglider is flawed by design: a wing with no rigid structure, which can collapse and let its pilot fall. “It’s stupid to fly something that will do that,” he writes[^c-masters-747]. He contrasts the paraglider with the hang glider, which has a frame, and accuses the paragliding community of being in denial. His messages have often been received as provocations, his statistics disputed, and the man himself rejected[^c-masters-forum].

Years later, I have to admit that his main arguments make sense. Rick Masters describes the paraglider as a *frameless* aircraft, and he drew what he calls the *Paraglider Dead Man’s Curve*[^c-masters-pdmc]: the band close to the ground where a collapse leaves no time either to recover the wing or to get a reserve open. On his diagram, the zone marked “AVOID” rises to more than a hundred metres for a simple collapse, and much higher for a spiral dive. In France in 2026, eight fatal accidents happened on cross-country flights after collapses close to the ground, with no reserve deployed: the pilots did not have time to throw it. One can reject Rick’s conclusions and his stubborn belief that his favourite aircraft (the hang glider) is safer… but his basic observation about the paraglider holds: there is a problem zone, and we lose pilots in it every year. NB: I know a reserve can sometimes open below 50 m above the ground. As we will see, I prefer to take the view that if, in practice, many pilots do not manage it, then we have an issue.

Rick Masters, like others who have taken on the community’s *mainstream* thinking, did not make many friends. His arguments were torn apart, the man mocked or insulted[^c-masters-forum2], his voice kept at a distance. And yet. In 2022, his count, which he himself describes as incomplete, reached 2,150 paraglider pilots dead since the first death he records, in 1986[^c-masters-impossible]. In 2023, he spoke of more than 2,250 deaths and compared the total to the passengers of several Boeing 747s[^c-masters-747]. In September 2024, his page “The problem with paragliding” read: “Rick Masters has verified 2,300+ paragliding deaths since the sport began. SOMETHING IS WRONG!”[^c-masters-2300]. One can argue about his method, his intentions and his conclusions. But he did what we have never done collectively: look at the number.

That may be the most uncomfortable lesson. It took a declared opponent of paragliding for someone to keep the list of our dead. And our reflex was to discredit the messenger rather than produce a better count ourselves. To ridicule and caricature arguments that still deserve careful analysis today.

### Scarce and closed data

For a few years now, things have been moving a little. Since 2017, the FFVL has published a list of the fatal accidents of its members, kept by the SecuParapente association in collaboration with the federation[^c-ffvl-dc]. It records 18 fatal accidents in 2023, 15 in 2024, 15 in 2025, and already 18 in 2026 by the end of October. Paragliding accounts for the vast majority of these deaths. Most happen in flight, not on take-off or landing. And supervised flying is not spared: in 2024, 8 of the 15 fatal accidents happened in a school or on a tandem. More than half. In 2025, 3 accidents in supervised flying killed 5 people: two of them killed both the pilot and the passenger. In 2026 there have again been 3, including a hang glider tandem accident that cost the lives of the pilot and the passenger. That sounds strangely at odds with the line “With proper supervision, there’s no risk”[^c-encadre]. The same list keeps a separate, non-exhaustive record of the deaths of non-member pilots on French territory. In 2026 there were 13 of them. Thirteen! That makes at least 31 free-flight deaths in France in ten months, more than four in ten of which escape federation statistics. A few federations publish an annual analysis of their accidents. The Swiss federation, for example, recorded 107 accident reports in 2025, 98 of them in paragliding, and 13 paragliding deaths out of 17 free-flight deaths. It points out itself that the real number of accidents is much higher than the number reported[^c-shv-2025]. The German federation (DHV), the European union of federations (EHPU) and, recently, even the International Hang Gliding and Paragliding Commission (CIVL) all have accident databases. The EHPU database is even shared: many European federations feed their reports into it, in a database developed by the DHV and rebuilt in 2023 under the name AIDA[^c-ehpu-aida].

Nevertheless, publications remain rare, and the efforts too recent and too scattered. Each federation counts in its own way, when it counts at all. The European database is restricted to member federations and their officials; it is not open to the public. There is no complete, public worldwide record, and most data remain closed.

The argument put forward is almost always the same: personal data protection. It is real, and it deserves to be taken seriously. But it does not stand up as a reason to publish nothing. Other high-risk communities publish without exposing anyone. Aviation accident investigation authorities publish detailed accident reports. The BASE jumping community has kept a public list of its dead since 1981, precise enough to serve as the basis for scientific work[^c-base]. Anonymised statistics, analyses by accident type and reports without names threaten no one’s privacy. What prevents publication is less the law than our culture.

### What accident reports tell us

The dead are only the visible part. FFVL members report more than 700 accidents every year: 728 in 2024 and 721 in 2025, over 90% of them in paragliding[^c-ffvl-export]. These are voluntary reports, most often made by the pilot or by their instructor. The real number of accidents is necessarily higher.

These reports tell us several things.

- **One accident in three is serious.** The pilot is seriously injured or killed in 242 of 728 reports in 2024, and in 231 of 721 in 2025.
- **One accident in two happens in supervised flying.** School, tandem, club or organisation: 367 reports out of 728 in 2024, 364 out of 721 in 2025. Tandem flying alone accounts for about 130 reports a year. These supervised accidents are, it must be said, markedly less serious: about one in five leads to serious injury or death, against nearly one in two in unsupervised flying.
- **We get hurt mostly near the ground, but we die mostly in the air.** Where the phase of flight is recorded, more than 40% of accidents happen on landing or on approach, about a third on take-off, and only one in five to one in four in flight. But of the deaths whose phase is known, 7 out of 10 in 2024 and 6 out of 7 in 2025 happened in flight.
- **Mid-air collisions are not rare.** 33 collisions with another paraglider or a hang glider were reported in 2024, and 22 in 2025.

At this point, the individual and social impact is far from negligible. It should first and foremost concern the victims of these accidents and the free-flying community as a whole. With that in mind, I tried to express these figures differently. Over a single year, and for a population of federation members, it is often hard to grasp what statistics mean. So let us try another way.

Here I must give credit where it is due. While rereading, I realised that Jean-Marc Galan, of the FFVL’s analysis unit, had already made this argument on the scale of a pilot’s career, in a video published in 2024[^c-galan-taux]. I redid it without knowing. That we each reached the same demonstration independently shows at least one thing: it is no fad.

### A pilot’s career

Let us relate these figures to the number of active pilots. In 2024, the FFVL had 14,989 active paraglider pilots, excluding trainees and first-year members, and 15,486 in 2025[^c-ffvl-licences]. In those same years, independent member pilots, that is excluding schools, tandem and SIV courses, reported 363 and then 365 accidents. Each year, therefore, about 2.4% of independent pilots report an accident, and about 1% are seriously injured or killed.

These percentages look small. However… if this risk stayed constant from one year to the next and we looked at it over a pilot’s lifetime, this is what would await us:

| Over a career of… | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| At least one reported accident | 1 pilot in 5 | 1 pilot in 3 (38%) | 1 pilot in 2 (52%) |
| At least one serious or fatal injury | 1 pilot in 10 | 1 pilot in 5 | 1 pilot in 4 |
| Killed paragliding | about 1 pilot in 170 | about 1 pilot in 90 | about 1 pilot in 60 |

In other words, a pilot who flies for thirty years has a better than even chance of reporting at least one accident, and a one-in-four chance of being seriously injured or killed. In a club of a hundred regular pilots, around twenty will be seriously injured or killed in twenty years, and one of them will probably lose their life.

These figures are conservative. Many accidents are never reported: if only one accident in two were, nearly two thirds of pilots would have at least one in twenty years. The calculation also assumes the same risk for everyone, whereas it probably varies with the type of flying, experience and commitment. Conversely, a few reported accidents involve pilots in their first year, who are not in the number of active pilots, which slightly inflates the rate. For deaths, the estimate rests on about twenty cases over two years: it gives an order of magnitude, not a precise measurement.

More broadly, we are working with small numbers: a few dozen deaths a year at most. From one year to the next, a difference of three or four deaths proves neither progress nor deterioration. Only trends over several years mean anything. That is also why incidents must be counted. The accident pyramid, proposed by Herbert Heinrich in 1931 and later taken up by Frank Bird, shows that for every serious accident there are dozens of minor accidents and hundreds of incidents without consequences[^c-pyramide]. Incidents are numerous: they are what give solid figures and show where the next serious fall is building up.

I often hear that our statistics are not very reliable because they deal with “small numbers”, since the FFVL receives only about 700 accident reports a year. It is the opposite. Seven hundred events a year is a solid sample: on such a number, chance alone moves the total by only a few per cent from one year to the next. The real weakness of these figures is not their number but what is missing from them: the accidents that are never reported. And those forgotten accidents can only make the toll heavier. Our statistics are a floor, not a ceiling. And if one fears they are distorted, because some accidents are reported more often than others, the answer is not to discard them but to report better.

And hang gliding? The community is much smaller: about 350 active pilots in the FFVL, excluding trainees and first-year members, counted the same way as for paragliding. Independent hang glider pilots reported 18 accidents in 2024 and 17 in 2025, 8 of them serious over the two years, and none fatal. Each year, then, about 5% of hang glider pilots report an accident, twice as many as in paragliding, and about 1.1% are seriously injured, almost as many as in paragliding.

| Over a career of… | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Hang gliding: at least one reported accident | 2 pilots in 5 | 2 pilots in 3 (64%) | 4 pilots in 5 |
| Paragliding: at least one reported accident | 1 pilot in 5 | 1 pilot in 3 (38%) | 1 pilot in 2 (52%) |
| Hang gliding: at least one serious or fatal injury | 1 pilot in 10 | 1 pilot in 5 | nearly 3 pilots in 10 |
| Paragliding: at least one serious or fatal injury | 1 pilot in 10 | 1 pilot in 5 | 1 pilot in 4 |

Hang glider pilots therefore have many more accidents, but on average they are less serious: about one accident in four is serious, against two in five in paragliding. This matches what we see in the field: most hang gliding accidents happen on landing, with a lot of broken equipment and often few injuries. These figures must, however, be read with great caution. With so few pilots, a few accidents more or less change everything: with 8 serious accidents in two years, the real annual rate could lie anywhere between 0.5% and 2.3%. As for the risk of death, only one fatal hang gliding accident was reported in two years, in supervised flying: far too few to derive a rate. We also remember sad series of accidents in previous years.

Presented this way, the figures seem implausible, but mathematics is what it is. If you have been paragliding for thirty years or so, on the scale of your little club, the most likely outcome is that you have already lost a dear member and friend. About a 1-in-60 misfortune. If that is not your case, then perhaps it is simply two in the neighbouring club. My competitive career took me through many small clubs, regional leagues, organisations and even a few countries. As you saw in the tribute, my denominator is unfortunately much larger than 60. Like many free-flight enthusiasts, I have lost more than one friend. For older pilots, calling them “survivors” of our activity is an exaggeration, but not much of one.

I often hear pilots object that you can die anywhere: driving, or even crossing the street on foot. That is true, but the order of magnitude is not at all the same. Over thirty years, a French person has about a one-in-700 chance of dying on the road, a pedestrian one in 5,000[^c-onisr-2024] and a motorcyclist about one in 170[^c-moto]. A paraglider pilot’s risk is closer to that of a fisherman, the deadliest occupation in France: about one chance in 47[^c-marins]. It is time to realise that paragliding does not replace life’s other risks; it adds to them. If you fly, you can still die on the road, and even crossing the street on foot.

### Not counting is already an answer

So I will not open this book with a table of definitive figures, because they do not exist. That gap is an answer in itself. A community that does not count its dead can go on believing its activity is safe. It can treat each accident as an isolated case, an individual mistake, one more “Pilotenfehler” (pilot error, as the DHV’s online summary of accident investigations puts it), a blunder then, or an exception. Our community never has to face the total.
