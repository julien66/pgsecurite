## Myth \#4

There are no flying conditions that are too strong in absolute terms: it is all a question of matching the pilot’s level, their equipment and their environment.

### A radical idea

This myth is simply a direct consequence of the first two. By dint of believing in the possibility of absolute control of the aircraft and mocking the incompetence of those who fail, we fall into this kind of extreme. “If you’re not dead, send it harder” is a popular saying in our community, supposed to be funny. It illustrates this radicalism. Images of extremely degraded flight situations that end well despite everything (a paraglider almost destroyed, or in a very unfavourable acro situation, that flies again anyway; apocalyptic conditions at the X-Alps) feed this imagination.


### Unflyable conditions

However, paragliding is an aerial activity physically bounded by the capacity of a fabric aircraft. If we can understand that a wing and its pilot cannot take on a cyclone, we must also admit that there is a range of conditions that mark a physical limit to flight. I have seen world-class paraglider pilots collapse and throw their reserve in gigantic fire thermals with recorded climbs above 20 metres per second, and others get sucked up several thousand metres into clouds that were a little too black. I have seen boiling flows in the lee of ridges smash wings one after another, rain heavy enough to ground whole packs of paragliders, and wind gradients or dust devils that are absolutely unflyable near the ground. A paraglider is not a magic broomstick. There is a physical limit that no one can push back, not even our favourite top pilot, not even an acro world champion. The situations described above cannot be managed; they can only be endured. They are beginning to be well documented, but they remain a frequent cause of accidents today.

The French federation itself made this observation without drawing the consequences. In 2015, which it calls a “black year”, it recorded, as of 31 October, 437 accident reports in paragliding and hang gliding, including 190 serious accidents with fractures and 19 fatal accidents. It attributes this rise to “this year’s exceptional weather, which made it possible to increase flying hours in conditions that were often strong or even difficult”. And it immediately adds: “we systematically find the same causes of accident as in previous years”[^c-ffvl-diaporama-2015]. Conditions change, accidents increase, but the lens remains the same: that of the pilot’s errors. We are now in 2026, ten years later. Awareness of global warming is becoming widespread, and we are living through another black year. How will we look back, at the end of the year, on what has just happened?

### Rudimentary equipment

We must face the facts and analyse a little more objectively what our equipment is. Let us put things as we would explain them to a layperson. At best, our paraglider has an airspeed range of roughly 25 to 65 kilometres per hour. The aircraft is an ultralight weighing less than 7 kilograms, unable to hold a flight level (in the aeronautical sense); it glides with an air glide ratio of between 5 and 11 depending on its speed, no more, no less. Its descent capability depends on the pilot’s training and is perhaps around an average of 15 metres per second using the 360-degree technique (spiral dive), at the cost of possibly drifting downwind and of high g-loads on both equipment and pilot. The machine is flexible so it can easily be carried, so it is understood that it can collapse under the effect of turbulence. When that happens, part of the wing fabric can get caught in the lines (cravat) and compromise the flight and/or the recovery of the aircraft. If the collapse is too violent, the two main risers can also cross one or more times in front of the pilot (twist), making recovery from the incident less likely. These events can of course combine (collapse, cravat, twisted pilot). As if that were not enough, stalling the aircraft (at low speed), which can be used to “clean up” many bad configurations, leads to complicated recoveries that must be skilfully piloted. The challenge is not to deform the wing too much outside the flight envelope, so as not to risk a cravat, while managing one’s timing to resume flight once the pendulum has stabilised, so as not to get too sharp a surge that would then be difficult to control. It goes without saying that the rescue system is manual. It has more or less not changed in 30 years. The reserve parachute can be hard to throw, get tangled in the main wing and become useless, or take a little too long to deploy if speed is low. Once under it, the story is not over; you still have to neutralise the main wing to avoid the downplane effect, which would cause too high a sink rate, and finally wait to get back to solid ground wherever the wind pushes you (power line, cliff, river, buildings included…) at a speed that is anyway rarely below 6 metres per second.

That is what our aircraft are, and I have not even mentioned harnesses. Seen from this angle, it becomes hard to hide how essentially rudimentary they are. Not an ounce of electronic flight assistance, no sensors, no autopilot. Almost constant active piloting: for most pilots, answering the radio or simply taking a photo is not always possible. Yet we love using our wings, or seeing them used, in limitless adventures. I am not quite sure how to compare what we do with the world of sailing, but I sometimes feel we are trying to cross the Atlantic Ocean the way Alain Bombard did[^15].

### Deceptive sturdiness

That is precisely what Rick Masters holds against the paraglider (see “Who counts our dead?”): a wing with no structure, *frameless*, which can collapse and let its pilot fall. To answer him, we even made up another little myth: the collapse is supposedly a safety feature[^m4-forum-fermeture]. If the wing can collapse, it is because it folds instead of breaking. With a little hindsight, the argument is completely illogical. It sets two failures against each other as if we had to choose between them, whereas a well-designed aircraft should experience neither within its flight envelope. Above all, it presents the collapse as a safety valve, whereas it is precisely the failure: a collapsed wing no longer flies, and the pilot falls. That it “does not break” makes no difference to them when it happens thirty metres above the ground. We turned the main flaw of our aircraft into a virtue, because someone who had put their finger on it had to be answered.

We must acknowledge, however, that today’s wings have little in common with those of the pioneers. In thirty years, comfort and performance have improved enormously. A modern wing feels taut, uniform, almost rigid. It absorbs turbulence that would have collapsed a 1990s wing, and it gives the pilot a feeling of stability and sturdiness their elders never knew. It is real progress, and it would be dishonest to deny it.

But this impression is partly deceptive. The fragility remains real: the aircraft is still flexible, still held up only by its internal pressure, and it can still collapse. What has changed is the way it collapses. The most telling example is the move to two-line wings, with only two rows of lines, A and B, instead of three or four. This design comes from competition. With fewer lines, rods and a better-held profile, the wing is harder to collapse. But when it does finally give way, the event is deeper. The collapse is bigger, more brutal, more energetic, and it probably also uses up more height. Incidentally, this height loss is not tested.

In other words, we have traded frequent and fairly benign incidents for rare and more serious ones. For more than 99% of a cross-country pilot’s flying time, nothing special happens any more. It is therefore a trade-off one can accept, but only if one knows about it. Perhaps a pilot who has never seen their wing collapse ends up believing it never will. In any case, the wing’s stability does not remove the risk: it makes it rarer, and therefore harder to imagine. And close to the ground, in the zone Rick Masters depicts with his *Dead Man’s Curve*, a deeper collapse leaves no margin at all (see “Who counts our dead?”). The community says wings are safer, whereas statistics show that they are more stable and more comfortable. Unfortunately, that has almost nothing to do with it.

### Rejecting chance and uncertainty

We often make fun of the uninitiated who talk about “air pockets” and “downdraughts”, because everyone in our community knows these do not exist and that nothing can turn a wing into a “streamer”… By extension, any mention of an exceptional or random threatening phenomenon is bound to meet with the greatest scepticism from our specialists. An unflyable thermal zone? A rotor in that spot? Shear, you say? Dubious stories; with the right technique you should get through! Even our best pilots say so (see Antoine Girard’s words in “The power of our beliefs”).

This time, I really believe our sailor friends went through a similar case with rogue waves before the 1990s and the first measurements and images. All the accounts, however consistent, were considered pure folklore, tall tales from poor captains who had not been able to handle their ship… In the end, science proved the existence of such waves, 2 to 3 (!) times bigger than the surrounding average, not always moving in the same direction, potentially breaking and not always arising from raging seas… In short, a pure delight of chaotic and random appearance, capable of damaging warships, and whose probability of occurring around the globe is roughly constant given the vast surface of the oceans. Science is now trying to strengthen predictive models on the subject. The equations are now known, and such phenomena can even be reproduced in laboratory wave tanks. Our aerological environment, all the more so at micro-scale, and its associated phenomena, especially near terrain, are neither studied, nor known, nor as predictable as we collectively seem to think. A paraglider is a very frail craft of which we ask a great deal, and more and more, in disturbed flows close to terrain. Airline pilots themselves communicate to avoid the most turbulent areas in real time. PIREPs (“Pilot Reports”), convective radar and SIGMET (“Significant Meteorological Information”) alerts are part of the arsenal available to the captain to stay out of trouble. If statistically extreme phenomena exist in certain rare conditions at sea or higher up in the sky, I am inclined to believe it can also happen in the convective layer we use.

To get to the point, what I mean is that perhaps, sometimes, one can get hammered without seeing it coming… who knows? In any case, I do not see birds flying in all weathers. Even under blue skies, I have seen birds of prey get flipped over in thermals and, on rarer occasions, I have seen some crash. Apparently, even for them, flying is not just a question of adapting to one’s environment!
