## Monitoring, alerting, rescuing

When an accident happens anyway, everything is decided in the minutes that follow: knowing it has happened, raising the alarm, coming to the rescue.

### Integrating weather stations into VHF (Very High Frequency)

Today, the network of weather stations is not optimally integrated either into VHF use or into our flight instruments. This source must be available in real time and accessible effortlessly in the air. Some areas would also benefit greatly from a system of VHF radio repeaters. In many situations, they make it possible to overcome the usual range problems caused by our low transmission power and over-short antennas in mountainous terrain. And speaking of VHF…

### Making better use of VHF

Radio does not have a very good image in free flight. It is seen as a rudimentary, old-fashioned tool, and many would like to replace it with something more modern, such as Zello or even the mobile phone network. The trend in some schools is to simplify, with basic monitoring walkie-talkies. VHF is clearly underused and underestimated. Most free-flying pilots know very little about the tool: change frequencies, roughly set the squelch and channel step, lock the keys, and that’s it!

This is a real shame, because a tool like DSC (Digital Selective Calling) would be more than useful for our activities. If you have ever imagined being able to send an automatic alert message from your radio on a specific dedicated channel straight to the rescue services, know that sailors did it before us. It warns the listening authorities and all nearby users of the same system. You can select the reason for the emergency call directly from the radio, and it will share your location to direct rescuers to your position.

In our case, the free-flying community’s work on this subject is still not enough to secure, once and for all, a specific frequency band for our aerial uses. Even at national level, our only frequency, 143.9875 MHz, rests on a fragile decree from 1993. And yet France is almost an exception. In many countries, free-flying pilots simply have no legal VHF. In the United Kingdom, paraglider pilots’ use of the two-metre band has never really been legal: the authorities have only agreed not to prosecute pilots. In Spain, you need a licence that most pilots do not have. Elsewhere, many fall back on PMR446 walkie-talkies, limited to half a watt[^c-globegliders]. In Switzerland, communicating legally by radio requires an aeronautical radiotelephony licence, obtained through powered-aircraft, glider or balloon training, and a radio registered in one’s name[^c-shv-funk]. In Germany, free-flying pilots have channels in the aviation band, but with a radiotelephony authorisation and an approved radio[^c-dhv-funk]. More than forty years after the first flights, our institutions have not known how, been able or been willing to get their states to recognise the most basic safety tool of our activity: being able to talk to each other in flight. While sailors and yachtsmen are in direct contact with the regional rescue coordination centres (CROSS in France) via a standardised, simplified international channel (16) and a dedicated phone number (196 in France), and while aviators call Mayday and Pan-Pan on 121.500 (the international distress frequency), paraglider pilots get by in other ways, and often alone.

We would gain by extending and professionalising the use of VHF. It is a fabulous, proven tool that has served air and maritime communications all over the world since the last century. Perhaps one fine day Starlink will make all this technology completely obsolete? In the meantime, having radios with the equivalent of an MMSI (Maritime Mobile Service Identity) and DSC establishing a direct emergency communication protocol with rescuers and other users, sending them coordinates, would not be superfluous. How much time is lost in communication? In collecting and sending coordinates? In misunderstandings about the location of a crash? How many accidents with no way of raising the alarm?

“Hang on, I’m landing. I’ll text you, share it with me on WhatsApp. Open the app. It’s my partner who got the message by email…” All this is ridiculous! When you think that all the rescue services (mountain rescue, firefighters, civil protection…) are also equipped with VHF and are just waiting to receive relayed information. This kind of development, and the lobbying that goes with it, is not beyond our community’s reach. In return, however, it may also mean training and taking a restricted radiotelephony certificate. Probably not so utopian to rely first on professionals and a few enthusiasts.

We hold a famous belief that VHF does not work well. “Because of the terrain.” But what I most often see in the field is a radio bought on sale at the lowest possible price, fitted with a dubious battery, a bent miniature antenna and a damaged antenna socket. If there is a remote set-up, it is most often a wired microphone whose connector clearly will not last long. That is the typical caricature, and it concerns the pilot who is equipped. Many pilots simply have no radio. Among instructors or organisers, who works at 25 watts with an extension cable and a good antenna? Which professional has equipped a landing field properly for the instructor guiding approaches (electricity, fixed radio, sunshade, water, comfortable seat…)? Apart from windsocks, the community more readily installs a bar or a barbecue than any other facility (radio relay, internet connection). It is a question of culture.

If you look at a student’s radio, the TOT (Time-Out Timer) is usually not set, the squelch barely so, and no one uses a tone or coded sub-channel to block interference from the main frequency, even though we know full well it is open to all comers. The details and *tips* of using this tool, indispensable to our activity, are missing from training content. Free-flying pilots do not like VHF because they do not know it and use it badly. Meanwhile, channel 16 and 121.500 save lives every day.

### A duty to listen. A duty to assist

I no longer wear canvas trainers to go flying; I switched to trail shoes long ago. I did not start wearing them for my own safety. I did it to stay useful just in case. Several times I have found myself rescuing injured friends on steep or slippery ground, or in trees. I have also had some bad experiences, because without the right equipment (phone, shoes, radio, battery…) or the right training (first aid, physical fitness, knots…), you are potentially useless to your fellow pilots. From the start, I understood that this sport could take a friend from me now and then, and I accepted it. That does not mean I will accept living with regret or negligence. I want to keep that strength and do my best. I will not go out partying the night before a flying day. I will do it on an evening when there will be no flying the next day. I charge the radios, the batteries and myself… We know what our activity involves. “With great power comes great responsibility,” said W. Churchill (or Spider-Man, depending). Let us do what is needed, all the more so when we are coaches and/or professionals.

Helping a person in danger is a legal obligation in most countries of the world. In my work for the FFVL, I have sometimes had to investigate fatal accidents. It is incredible how, in most cases, pilots who went flying together do not realise that one of them has had an accident. What is the VHF listening protocol used in free flight? There is none. How do we locate each other? Most of the time, pilots do not. What would you do if one of your friends stopped answering on the radio and their position showed them on the ground in difficult terrain? If this question has an obvious answer (“I call the rescue services”), then we could easily give ourselves the means to have this information: tracking and radio contact.

The community is fascinated by distance records and the X-Alps. Yet many seem to forget that during these events, safety protocols are very precise and rely on a large collective effort. Athletes are generally in constant contact with their supporter(s) and are followed continuously on live tracking. Sometimes the supporter sitting behind a screen does it from home; sometimes from the athlete’s van during the race. Often there are several of them, remote and on site! Of course, tracking stays on all the time and emergency numbers are known. It may not be very “Mike Horn”, but it is very reasonable. Even in the Vendée Globe, competitors give themselves the means to keep watch and assist one another. I guarantee that these protocols take nothing away from the pleasure of flying all day over 460+ km. Quite the opposite: it is very pleasant and liberating to know that everyone’s safety will be taken care of if something goes wrong.

The duty to listen and assist at sea consists of committing to listen to the same radio frequency in order to be able to help if necessary. Of course, the context is different, notably in that:

- Victims have more time on the water. Boats generally do not sink instantly. Almost all shipwrecked people can be saved, at least for a while. A paragliding accident unfolds on a different timescale, and there are many more cases where victims cannot raise the alarm themselves and where rescue operations will change nothing.

- On the water, there is an urgent need to intervene immediately, because the ocean or the sea may swallow everything if rescuers arrive too late (lost with all hands). On land, this urgency is less pronounced.

Even so, how can one not be won over by the idea of a community committed to keeping its own watch and assisting itself in the face of the risk and peril of its shared passion? Sailors, but also mountaineers, sometimes try to go and fetch their unfortunate friends, stuck or in a bad way, at the risk of their own lives! I want to believe our community is made of the same stuff. I have investigated fatal accidents where a pilot crashes and stays alone on the ground for hours while their friends fly over them in a thermal ten minutes later. This is not an exception; I am talking about a recurring, repeatedly documented scenario. Most of the time, spotting from the air is difficult. Making out a wing without bright colours in the middle of the mountains is a delicate exercise, even for a helicopter pilot or an experienced rescuer. And believe it or not, in some cases even with roughly the right coordinates! If free-flying pilots do not stop to help their fallen friends, it is not out of selfishness or individualism. Sometimes they do not see them, and they assume that in the absence of visual proof of an incident, all is well. Afterwards, 100% of the friends are shocked and blame themselves for missing the right information. Almost every time, we reassure ourselves by saying it would not have changed anything, that the impact or the accident was too severe, that the victim died instantly. That is true most of the time, and yet across all cases it is not always accurate. It is a relief to think it, to say it, and it frees everyone from guilt. Nothing will bring back our fallen friends. In truth, what is wrong is that we are in the 21st century but we still fly without a defined radio protocol and without any automatic distress system.

### Volunteer rescue

Another borrowing from the world of sailing! While civilian and military staff coordinate the rescue system via the CROSS centres, operationally they mobilise a veritable army of volunteers from the French national sea rescue society (SNSM), which has more than 200 lifeboat stations in France. In the absence of such an organisation to watch over an entire coastline, I think we could still organise a few watch rotas ourselves in our major leisure flying areas.

At our latitudes, the paragliding season starts in spring and continues through summer before gently fading with the arrival of autumn and then winter. Nevertheless, the community loves to travel, and the season increasingly looks like a series of ritual gatherings at famous spots around the globe: September in Val di Fassa in Italy, October in Ölüdeniz in Turkey, November in Bir in India, December in Tenerife, January–February in Colombia in the Valle del Cauca or in Mexico at Valle de Bravo, and so on. Often the flying area hosts over 300 leisure pilots a day. Everyone flies and lives at the local rhythm. Probably no one has ever devoted a single day of their time to monitoring and coordinating rescue for friends who have gone flying. How could they? Absolutely nothing is planned for it. In the most exotic countries, where coordinating rescue and raising the alarm are still complex or unusual processes, we could organise temporary stations equipped with a radio and an emergency number, providing a direct link with the authorities. Having a couple of trained volunteers give one day of their stay would be rewarding. We would probably even be happy to fund this kind of initiative if we had to and if it were undertaken locally. We already pay for plane tickets, accommodation, site fees and lifts to launch, not to mention post-flight beers. We must realise that this kind of rescue system would serve to protect free-flying pilots. That is, ourselves. Carrying on as if everything will be fine when we know, year after year, week after week or day after day, that it is not the case, is irresponsible.

Along the same lines, would it be hard to imagine a couple of volunteers dedicated to watching live tracking on good days? With a PureTrack-type platform dedicated to detecting, checking and alerting? With other FFVL coaches, we spend a lot of time watching pilots’ performances and tracks across different areas. Several times now we have tried to find out how a pilot visibly in trouble on the tracking was doing. Finding their number, contacting them, checking, and if necessary raising the alarm and then contacting their family is never a small matter. The feeling of helplessness is sometimes terrible and turns an apparently harmless experience into a nightmare. Yet the community can decide to organise this monitoring very easily. It takes a little time, willingness and a few protocols, nothing more. I am lucky to have access to a federation intranet with phone numbers and emergency contacts for many pilots. It has already come in very handy several times. What will you do when you see me crash live during the French team’s next distance record attempt? Whom will you call? How will you raise the alarm hundreds or thousands of kilometres from my crash? These are questions we can and must answer quickly.
