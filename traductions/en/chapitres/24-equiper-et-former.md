## Equipping and training

Next come the sites, the equipment, and what the pilot carries and learns, depending on the type of flying they have chosen.

### Equipping flying sites

Equipping flying sites is a serious matter, including for safety. Of course, thousands of sites are completely wild. With no agreement in place, using them offers nothing but a wonderful natural space to lay out a wing and take off. But for sites with an agreement, that is, those where there is some form of arrangement with the landowners, where traffic is heavier and, all the more so, those used by professionals, we can optimise our organisation and mutual support.

Examples of questions to ask, depending on traffic, might be: what space is set aside for pre-flight checks? Is there a hang point available to adjust new equipment? What are the practical options for getting back down if you decide not to fly? Are the take-off and landing procedures clearly signposted? Are nearby airspaces identified? On coastal sites or above a lake, the risk of a water landing should be explicitly mentioned and assessed: wind direction relative to the shore, distance to the water, whether or not there is any watch, the possibility of being picked up quickly. In 2024 and 2025, 11 water landings were reported to the FFVL, 3 of them fatal, spread across 7 départements, 5 of them in Haute-Savoie[^c-ffvl-amerrissage]. Having some form of site labelling (e.g. “Free-flight stadium” / “Major free-flight site”) and a standard to meet according to actual traffic or in proportion to the number of professionals would provide good benchmarks.

### Equipment and personal protective equipment (PPE)

Absurd as it may seem, the colour of the wing affects how visible a potential accident is and how effective any search operation can be. Blue (sky), white (snow), green (forest), beige (earth) and black (rock) are colours that easily blend into the environment. Bright colours, shades of red and yellow, work better in most terrain. Before blithely dismissing this systemic argument (“well, just buy a red wing then”), it is worth noting that even in areas particularly well served by helicopter rescue, such as the Pyrenees or the Hautes-Alpes, paraglider pilots are sometimes searched for for weeks.

This section will not, however, stop at cosmetics; it intends to go into the detail of our fabulous aircraft, from design to production and testing. The community likes to remind anyone who will listen that equipment-related accidents no longer exist. This assertion is plain wrong, and in more ways than one. Even considering an equipment accident strictly as a structural defect, a part that “breaks” during the flight, some recent accidents still fall squarely into this category. A maillon, a tandem spreader bar, a reserve bridle, lines, attachment tabs… Of course some of us are still sometimes affected by breakage! It is terribly bad luck, of course. Wear, improper use, but also production problems can lead to errors and ultimately to equipment failures in flight. It is quite marginal, but when it happens, it counts. Manufacturers regularly issue recalls, to the point of pushing a few famous companies close to bankruptcy.

It is as if we forgot that quality control is carried out by the manufacturer itself. There is no individual checking intermediary between the manufacturer/producer and the consumer/pilot. Someone at the factory usually declares your wing compliant and fit for sale. These industrial processes are internal. Generally they are perfect, but I have received a new machine with a line 7 cm too long and another with brake lines far too short.

The very structure of your wing is probably laser-cut but is still sewn by hand. In competition, we know it is hard to have two identical wings. The smallest details affect speed and behaviour. And here I am talking about manufacturers who do the work, who design, test and certify. Nothing obliges them to. In most countries of the world, that is, outside the German-speaking market (which is more regulated), nothing obliges a manufacturer to do anything. It can outsource design, production and testing, and can even decide not to have its equipment certified at all.

Not so long ago, I saw a video on Instagram of a poor pilot who takes off perfectly from a grassy summit, glides 100 m above a ski slope and gets a total frontal collapse at 50 m above the ground with no perceptible warning. She crashes without much injury but badly shaken. Of course, the comments online lynch her at length.

- “You should’ve braked…”
- “Why didn’t you counter?”
- “Go do an SIV course”

Personally, I cannot quite see what she did wrong. In fact, it turns out the wing was a small, uncertified size from an otherwise reputable brand, which she will probably never fly again. Stories like this are legion. The extreme sizes (XXS and XXL) of wing models (even popular ones) are produced by scaling. The M size, or the one the research and development team can fly, gets all the effort, while the other sizes are generally derived from the main one. Obviously, the further you move away from the sizes specially developed by the designer and their test pilots, the worse the result. Not only are the smallest (or largest) sizes, for which the market is not big enough, not tested, they are not certified either. And the same goes for all products that struggle to fit the current definition of the standard.

Where they exist, certification tests have significant limitations. The current EN standard for wings and harnesses is defined by a working group of experts called Working Group 6 – WG6.

Its full name is CEN/TC 136/WG 6: it is the “paragliding equipment” group of the technical committee devoted to sports within the European Committee for Standardization (CEN)[^c-cen-wg6]. It brings together representatives of manufacturers, testing laboratories and national federations. AFNOR coordinates its work and the EHPU funds the administrative side. It drafts the standards EN 926-1 (wing strength), EN 926-2 (flight behaviour), EN 1651 (harnesses) and EN 12491 (reserve parachutes)[^c-wg6]. These standards are voluntary: a manufacturer can choose not to submit its equipment to them.

I should point out that I have recently joined it myself, for the FFVL. The guide I mention below was written before I arrived. What follows is based on EN 926-2:2013. The text has been amended many times since, but the test conditions described here remain essentially the same.

These tests have limitations that must be known. They are carried out on a new wing, in calm air, by a test pilot sitting in a harness of prescribed dimensions. The standard spells it out: wind below 10 km/h and “no turbulence in the test area likely to disturb the flight tests”[^c-en926-2]. The test pilot induces incidents in a standardised way. A collapse triggered by hand above a lake is nothing like a collapse caused by an end-of-day thermal right next to a ridge. The classification therefore describes how a wing behaves during codified manoeuvres, not how it behaves in real turbulence. Nor does it say anything about how the wing changes over time: fabric porosity, line stretch, trim drift.

It is not me saying so; it is WG6 itself. In a guide intended for pilots, it presents EN classification as “a guide, but not a guarantee”. It writes that “it is not possible to reliably and reproducibly recreate real-world turbulence”, that the standard “does not measure passive safety in real-world scenarios such as thermals, wind shear or low-level turbulence”, and that the manoeuvres tested “represent only a small sample of the incidents that can occur in flight”. It adds that, even in calm air, a wing may react more severely than in the tests. We also learn that the tests are done in a seated harness, whereas many of us now fly in a pod[^c-wg6].

Then the same text concludes: “Accidents are rarely due to shortcomings in testing; they are mainly due to human error, poor decisions or lack of preparation by pilots.” What is this claim based on? No data are cited. And for good reason: we have seen that our report forms can hardly record an equipment factor, and that none asks whether the situation went beyond what the standard tests (see “What our forms cannot record”). The document that has just explained that the standard says nothing about how a wing behaves in real turbulence then claims that this gap causes almost no accidents. Myth number two is there, intact, from the pen of those who write the standard: when it breaks, it’s the pilot.

None of this takes anything away from the value of the tests. They remain an essential slice of the Reason model. But a slice has holes, and we should show them rather than let people believe that a certified wing is “forgiving” in all circumstances. A few ideas:

- Clearly indicate, at the point of sale, sizes and models that are not certified.
- Publish test reports in a form an average pilot can read.
- Encourage regular equipment checks and send reminders when they are due.
- Report manufacturing defects into the same database as accidents.

### Performance, comfort, lightness: what the market chose

In forty years, the transformation of our equipment has been spectacular. But looked at closely, it has almost entirely concerned three areas: performance, comfort and lightness. Each of them has been heavily invested in by manufacturers and widely endorsed by pilots, who confirm these directions every time they buy, or do not buy, a product. Safety, for its part, has almost never been an area in its own right. Everyone uses it as an argument, but it has never sold anything.

A few examples are enough to show it.

- **Wings.** They are said to be ever safer, but what they have mainly gained is performance, comfort and lightness, including where it is not really necessary. Even school wings today glide far better than a student needs for their first flights. Performance wings are more stable, more comfortable, lighter and better-performing, but not safer (see “Deceptive sturdiness”).
- **Protection.** When we discover a stronger or more absorbent material, we do not simply replace the existing protection. We take the opportunity to reduce the size and thickness of the component. The new protection then passes the same tests and protects as well as the old one, not better. What we have gained is weight and slimness.
- **The reserve parachute.** Its shape and materials have evolved, but its principle has not changed: it is still deployed by hand, by a pilot who must still have the time, the height and the clarity of mind to throw it (see “Rudimentary equipment”).
- **Coastal soaring.** We know that a pilot who falls into the water risks drowning under their wing (see “Knowing our accidents”). Yet no manufacturer has ever built a self-inflating life jacket into a harness to make a product dedicated to soaring.

Manufacturers have only followed and reinforced a market trend. It is consumers, magazines and influencers who decide what sells. A gain in glide can be measured, compared and demonstrated in flight, in competition or on social media. A gain in safety cannot be seen, except on the day it is needed, and on that day no one talks about it.

We lack information that no one produces. We have never kept statistics on which brands and models are actually the safest, in proportion to the number of units sold. Manufacturers know their sales, federations receive accident reports, but no one cross-references the two. Yet that would be the least we could do. It is also the only way to make safety a verifiable selling point, rather than a promise everyone makes.

### Safety equipment suited to each type of flying

Equipment does not stop at the wing and harness. Once in the air, the pilot is alone, and what they have brought along often decides the outcome of an incident. Most of these items may never be used. But on the day you need them, it is too late to go and get them.

Yet we talk about “the pilot’s equipment” as if there were only one. Because we do not separate types of flying, we cannot agree on what is necessary in general. Electronic conspicuity and trackers are the perfect example: useless for local flying by the sea, indispensable for cross-country in the Pyrenees. As long as we look for a single rule, we find none. Safety equipment is directly linked to the type of flying: you do not equip yourself the same way for evening flights on your home site, for a big mountain cross-country or for a para-alpinism traverse. A safety system worthy of the name should say so clearly, type by type, and teach it from the very start of training.

Once again, the world of sailing seems to have had a good idea in distinguishing the required safety equipment according to the type of navigation and the distance from shelter. That is exactly what we could take inspiration from. To stay safe on the high seas, you need more equipment than within 6 nautical miles of the coast.

I propose an equipment list that is not entirely far-fetched but remains hypothetical. This work deserves a broad debate within the federation and the creation of a shared consensus, first on the definition of the spheres of activity and then on the essential equipment. Adopting this kind of collective agreement will not be the work of one man. We will need to come together to agree on what makes sense, and that is what we cannot do as long as we do not separate the needs of different types of flying.

**The baseline: site flying.** As long as the pilot stays within glide of a landing field, they carry:

- certified equipment: wing, harness, helmet;
- a reserve parachute, for the day the wing stops flying;
- a vario and a GPS;
- a VHF radio to listen and to raise the alarm.

**Coastal soaring.** Over water, the danger changes in nature. To the baseline are added:

- a self-inflating life jacket;
- a hook knife, which is vital: in the water, the wing and its lines can drag the pilot down and drown them.

**Cross-country.** As soon as the pilot leaves the safety of the site’s landing field, they may land anywhere: in a tree, in an isolated gully, far from any rescue. To the baseline are added:

- an electronic conspicuity device, to be seen by other aircraft (see below);
- a satellite tracker, to be found where the phone no longer works;
- a compass, to hold a heading in a cloud;
- a rope and a descender, to get down from a tree or a cliff band;
- a hook knife to free oneself if needed;
- a space blanket, to wait for rescue.

**Para-alpinism.** Here the logic is reversed. The para-alpinist carries everything on their back, on foot as in flight, and every gram counts: they want to fly light because they move through mountainous terrain where they must stay agile. Their equipment is therefore reduced to the bare minimum:

- a satellite tracker;
- a compass to hold a heading if they get swallowed by a cloud.

**Acro.** To the baseline are added:

- a hook knife: here too, being able to cut a line free is essential;
- a second reserve parachute: in acro, pilots throw more often, and sometimes in difficult conditions. Let us carry a backup.

### Being seen: electronic conspicuity between aircraft

In general, as long as we are circling close to the ground above our site, we mostly share the sky among ourselves. As soon as we go cross-country, we enter a space that others also use: gliders, microlights, light aircraft, rescue or military helicopters flying low. Their speed has nothing in common with ours. A glider spiralling at 120 km/h, a helicopter flying up a valley: at these speeds, the “see and avoid” principle on which our visual flight rules are based leaves only a few seconds. And a wing seen head-on, against a mountain background, is one of the hardest things to spot in the sky.

Others understood this before us. Gliding has equipped itself massively with FLARM, a system that broadcasts the aircraft’s position and alerts the pilot when a trajectory threatens to cross their own. Free flight now has several solutions: some instruments include a FLARM transmitter, others the open FANET protocol, and phone apps relay the pilot’s position to other users. Networks of ground receivers, such as the Open Glider Network, make these positions visible in real time, which also helps to find a pilot after an accident.

The problem is that these systems do not always talk to each other. A FANET-only device is not compatible with FLARM; only some instruments known as “FANET+” combine the two, to varying degrees. FLARM and ADS-B, the surveillance standard of commercial aviation and much of general aviation, are two separate ecosystems. A new lightweight standard, ADS-L, designed for low-cost, low-power devices, is being promoted in light aviation and free flight, with a dedicated plan in Switzerland (FASST-CH), but it does not replace the others[^c-flyvisible]. Being equipped only protects you if the other aircraft is too, and speaks the same language. There are also limits specific to our activity: in a gaggle of twenty wings in a thermal, an alert system designed for gliders would sound non-stop. What matters for us is above all to be seen.

So this is a collective project more than an individual one. Federations, instrument manufacturers and other airspace users need to agree on a common standard, or at least on bridges between existing standards. We must also clearly recommend, from the pilot rating onwards, that a pilot going cross-country carries an electronic conspicuity device, just like their tracker.

### Training suited to the flying environment

The same logic applies to training. We train “the pilot” as if there were only one type of flying, whereas the essential knowledge changes with the environment. The pilot going cross-country independently crosses airspaces, meets other aircraft and may need to call for help far from everything. For them, a real knowledge of airspace seems indispensable, as does the use of the radio. For the pilot soaring by the sea or flying locally, within glide of a landing field, this is much less the case, or not at all. On the other hand, by the sea, you need to know how to prepare a water landing and be able to get out of your equipment quickly once in the water.

Rather than imposing a single package on everyone, often too heavy for some and inadequate for others, we could build training like equipment: a common core, then modules specific to each type of flying, which the pilot completes when they need them. In this core, first-aid training for all does not seem superfluous given the statistics: more than 700 accidents reported every year, one in three of them serious or fatal, and one pilot in five seriously injured or killed over twenty years of flying (see “Who counts our dead?”). On a launch, on a landing field or at the foot of a tree, the first rescuer is almost always another pilot. Knowing how to raise the alarm is crucial: you need to know whom to call, obtain and give a precise position, describe the injured person’s condition and the terrain, and stay reachable.

The training pathway could therefore be reviewed according to the type of flying targeted and the level of autonomy sought. At sea, the coastal licence is not the same training as the offshore licence. Operating a rescue device, securing yourself in a tree, getting out of the water: these skills are not equally useful everywhere. Today, the sequence of initial rating, pilot rating and advanced pilot rating is probably too general. I can be advanced in coastal soaring without being so in mountain flying, and vice versa. The idea is not to multiply ratings: the existing levels and colours can be kept. But training can evolve towards more modularity and individualisation.


### A charter rather than an obligation


In free flight, neither the equipment list by type of flying nor the tailored training are meant to become obligations. The idea is not to constrain, but to write them into a federation charter. A charter is a collective agreement: that of members who share a vision of what the activity should be and of how to access it. Everyone remains free to ignore it, but its use provides the framework and the common sense of direction. The pilot who goes cross-country without a tracker or knowledge of the regulations commits no offence as long as they do not actually enter controlled airspace. They simply know that they are departing from what the community considers reasonable practice, and that if things went really badly wrong, it would then be harder for them to plead ignorance.

Breaking the charter carries no penalty. Should we therefore believe it has no effect? That is not what I see in the field. Once safety practices are accepted, the community is on the contrary quite fierce in defending them itself. Post a nice video of yourself flying without a helmet on Instagram, and you will see! As a reminder, no regulation requires the independent pilot to wear a helmet. It is only required in schools, for tandem passengers, in competition, and where a prefectoral order requires it[^c-ffvl-casques]. And yet almost no one flies without one. Peer pressure does what regulation does not fully do.

I lived through this transition phase with satellite trackers. At the end of 2021 and the start of 2022, after COVID, the FFVL national team had a large budget, since we had hardly been able to travel. I had 75 SPOT (Globalstar) trackers bought to equip every pilot in our sports performance programme. The federation paid for the devices and the first year’s subscriptions, and we handed them out, convinced we had done something great. The truth is that it took years for our athletes to use them. Very few saw the point. Pilots do not crash that often, and many thought that on the day it happened, it would be really unlucky not to have phone signal, all the more so in Europe or in the Alps. Today, many no longer go out without one, even outside official training. It takes time, but once the habit is formed, there is no going back.

A charter relies on that strength: it does not punish; it gives the community a benchmark and a common sense of direction to defend.

A charter has another advantage over regulation: it can evolve quickly. It follows the activity, the people taking part and the knowledge we accumulate about our disciplines, without waiting for a reform. It is probably the form most faithful to the spirit of free flight: a shared commitment rather than an imposed rule, which preserves the pilot’s freedom while telling them clearly what we know.
