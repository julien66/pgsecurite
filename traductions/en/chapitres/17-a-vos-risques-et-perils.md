## At your own risk and peril

Here I want to look at the first myths discussed in the light of a notion our community abhors: that of peril. Peril differs from risk in that it cannot be anticipated and is not inherently controllable. As Bastien Soulé writes[^17], “one takes a risk; one suffers a peril”.

We never talk about this concept, and we struggle to perceive the perils of paragliding, even though there are some. Perhaps the reader could pause and try to guess even one? What peril, during an ordinary flight, can cause a fatal accident while escaping any form of control or mastery by the pilot? Generally, we are so stuck in the paradigm of a sport with controllable risk that we no longer even see the real perils. From our pilot’s point of view, everything seems rationally controllable and avoidable.

Yet when thermalling in a gaggle, even without counting 90 wings, a minor error by a single pilot is enough to take a couple of colleagues nearby into a bad collision, or worse. And it happens more and more… Let us put ourselves for two seconds in the place of the pilot calmly circling while another one falls on them, wing in a mess. Where is the error this time? Perhaps the one falling could have done better, but the other one? It is a peril, like a heavy stone falling from the mountain at the wrong moment, in the wrong place. No one can anticipate or prevent the collapse of someone else’s paraglider, or a surprising and inappropriate line in a big gaggle. The only way to protect oneself would have been not to take up the activity, or to stay away from everyone.

Even supposing it were still possible to fly while being completely obsessive, pilots would not be spared peril. I have seen collisions with birds, and I have studied how pilots hit cables that are almost invisible from the sky. A fainting spell that would have been harmless on the ground sends a pilot straight into the sea. A dust devil, while preparing to launch with the wing barely laid out, lifts the pilot and then slams them back down. Even the physicists who study them can only measure them in the field when a vortex happens to pass over their sensors. In the laboratory, they arise spontaneously, simply from heating of the ground, without any trigger. Winds of up to 25 m/s appearing out of nowhere: that is the very definition of a peril[^c-dustdevil]. A strong, sudden thermal release while flying along a ridge is not so fundamentally different. A big gust in the middle of an inflation or while picking up speed on the speed bar. The wind reversing strongly on landing while the pilot is behind the field, on base leg, as a big thermal or a breeze front goes through.

It is an illusion to think you can control all that. Sometimes you will pay the consequences of your commitment to this activity, even if it is not your fault. We are subject to perils. Just because they are less visible (air is invisible) does not mean they do not exist. Indeed, many pilots tell me about the ambiguous feelings they have towards free flight: an activity that is at once magnificent, indispensable and so frightening. Many federation members are not just a little scared in the air: they also develop forms of anxiety at the thought of going flying. You surely know some. When this happens, the main reason is generally that the body and the unconscious know full well that we fly not only managing (controllable) risks but also facing perils. Our “intelligence” convinces us that it is only a matter of managing the former, and it is precisely on this conflict that anxiety feeds. It can be cured over the medium term by working on acceptance: “OK, today I am going to do something that is not just a little risky. I need it, and I want to do it for this reason.”

Generally, after a few weeks, the anxious phases subside, because the basic premise is clearer and more honest. Yes, something can happen to you. No, it will not necessarily be your fault. Paradoxically, this idea allows a kind of relaxation and focus on the essentials. Not everything depends on you: if you manage the risks you perceive in the situation, that will already be good. At top level, in gaggles of 120 pilots at the start, we manage quite a few risks, but we accept many more perils. It is dense, it is turbulent, wings brush against each other a little. Obviously it is potentially fatal, but I cannot focus on it obsessively: I accept it. Yes, someone may fall on me. I manage my own margins, and I trust the others, the thermal and the wind so that everyone can do the same. In competitive group flying, you accept the peril or you leave the gaggle, with reduced performance.

Very experienced pilots are generally unable to perceive perils. Not only do they not see them, but they refuse to believe in them when shown concrete examples. Their reflex is always the same: they search everywhere for what the pilot could have done to protect themselves. A gaggle? “You don’t have to go in…” A dust devil? “It’s not that bad, you just have to choose your moment to launch…” A wind shift on landing? “You can anticipate that! You just have to turn…”

Peril has been erased from our vocabulary because it ticks none of the boxes of our community myths. If there is peril, the activity cannot be completely controllable (myth \#1)… And accidents could also happen for external or even random causes, independent of pilots’ errors (myth \#2). That would make the sky a place where one cannot fully adapt to overcome the difficulties found there (myth \#4)…

All this is rather problematic, and peril is an embarrassment! So we hide it. Whereas we could bear in mind that a simple vasovagal faint, in our aerial context, sometimes ends in tragedy where it would have had no real consequence on the ground. If we decided to talk about it more, myth \#1 of a safe activity would be harder to maintain.

In my role as a coach, I had decided to say that this kind of thing was part of the game. I told pilots to choose whether or not to accept the “risk” inherent in our game, that it was necessary for performance. In fact, I now realise that I was sometimes talking about perils… and that I can protect neither myself nor them from what we do. The overwhelming majority of our young athletes and their families accept this. They do as the grown-ups do. We train them and help them through the stages in order to overcome an apprehension that is instinctively entirely justified. A few exceptions do not get past this point. These young athletes then lose any chance of breaking through at top level, and we have to support them in leaving the programme and changing course. Taking risks and exposing oneself to perils in full awareness is not such a common choice, and it should be shared more explicitly with everyone involved. It is the kind of decision that must be taken in full awareness and without pretence.

I never used the word “peril”, but I had in mind a kind of distinction between risk activities on the one hand and extreme activities on the other. For a long time I believed that as soon as an activity involved an uncontrollable element, it was an extreme activity. I went as far as classifying paragliding as a risk activity and riding a motorcycle as an extreme activity. Because it was understood that motorcyclists are often exposed to the mistakes of car drivers. It was a classification I often referred to, convinced I had learned it somewhere (Risk sport -\> Controllable / Extreme sport -\> Danger suffered). I have discovered that no such classification exists in the literature, and how illusory control is.

You will still hear me say that paragliding is an extreme sport. I know (again thanks to Bastien Soulé) that the term has no academic rigour and is above all a marketing concept. The original “extreme” games (or X Games) were a franchise built around skateboarding, inline skating and BMX… Nevertheless, I want to help raise the perceived risk around our discipline, and the word “extreme” is now widely understood in that sense. An activity in which you can die. A sport where your life is at stake.
