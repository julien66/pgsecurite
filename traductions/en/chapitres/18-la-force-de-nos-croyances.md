## The power of our beliefs

Where do these beliefs we have taken the time to dissect come from? How were they built? Why do they persist? There is a strong convergence of interests in promoting the central idea, number one in our earlier discussion, of safe paragliding with controllable risk.

### A history centred on justifying the activity’s safety

That is, first, the direction of history. The pioneers really did have to learn to master their aircraft and their own activity. They knew little about their environment and about aerology. It is hard for us to remember where we came from fifty years ago, but the first hang gliders were delivered as kits to assemble yourself, sometimes with a single A4 sheet of instructions as the only explanation. Some pioneers even designed and built their own hang glider, paraglider or tandem. The only possible way to learn was by apprenticeship with someone who had already done a little, or by trial and error, often among friends. No wonder, in this context, that the desire for control is central to the collective imagination of free-flying pilots.

“It was risky back then; we knew nothing and we did any old thing.”
“If there was really too much wind on one side of the mountain, we’d walk a bit down the other side to take off in the shelter.”
“We’re survivors.”

These accounts invite comparison with the undeniable safety effort that has indeed been made over the past few decades. This effort was a necessity: it had to be shown that the activity perhaps involved a few risks… but that they were controllable! As a community, we made this effort to the point of convincing ourselves.

Indeed, the old-timers sensed long before us how society imposes a demand for sanitisation on people who practise risk activities. It is the free-flying pilots themselves who are called upon to demonstrate that their activity is safe, or risk having their new toys banned outright. The state also very quickly raises the question of how these activities are insured for the damage they cause. Who covers this risk? At what price? Who has an interest in doing so if the discipline is nothing but a form of open-air Russian roulette? Even today, third-party aviation liability insurance is a central issue and perhaps the only form of regulation of free flight broadly shared by most nations of the world.

Institutionalisation usually responds to this need for safety orthodoxy (a concept well known in physical education, which I borrow from Pierre Arnaud[^18]). The aim is to gain a form of legitimacy, and the parachuting, gliding and free-flight federations logically seek to bring together, represent and defend the interests of their members. Making the activity safe is of major strategic interest for developing and democratising hang gliding and paragliding in the manner of a sports federation. An activity that appeared unsafe, all the more so in the air with an aircraft the participant could not fully control, does not fit the sporting doxa and risks being banned or marginalised. BASE jumping and wingsuit flying are a perfect illustration of this today, and are following the path taken by the first hang glider pilots and later by the band of friends having fun jumping off the mountain at Mieussy with pseudo-parachutes. Every community born around “extreme” (dangerous) ideas finds itself obliged to show its credentials and to organise itself to make its discipline credible in terms of safety.

In hang gliding as in paragliding, efforts around teaching and learning were immediate. Very early on, it became possible in practice to “learn” the activity through courses in schools. Wonderful teaching books appeared, and with them the first qualified instructors, who later structured the federation pathways. In France, pilot ratings appeared. A training pathway was born, covering everything from introduction to advanced level, with clearly identified instructors and specialists. All over the country, professionals are wonderful catalysts for the activity. They raise awareness, evangelise, and often even convert a wide public to free flight.

On the equipment side, the dynamic is similar. Although manufacturers have been praising the stability of their products since day one, the fact is that their products objectively improve and are increasingly subjected to tests carried out by independent laboratories. EN and LTF certification, and even the CCC standard in competition, are all attempts to rationalise and make more reliable the equipment available to pilots according to their level.

In reality, a great many developments in the history of free flight can be read through the prism of making the activity safer and keeping its participants safe. To the point that one can actually believe the pioneer era is over and that paragliding is now a safe activity in which pilots can control the risks. The myth of the reliable activity was born first to answer the main criticism levelled at the discipline. It is an attempt and a reaction. For the anthropologist and historian René Girard[^19]: “If there is a normal order in societies, it must be the fruit of an earlier crisis; it must be the resolution of that crisis.” We have seen that myths are part of this normal order of things, and my main hypothesis is that we have come to believe firmly in a job that is in fact still in progress.

### A reassuring imagination

The good results of commercial aviation give a reassuring impression at the same time. As the journalist Malcolm Gladwell analyses[^20], accidents are considered exceptions. It really is possible to fly safely, and is the aeroplane not, after all, the safest means of transport in the world?

Our imagination does not include in-flight incidents and leaves no room for accidents once the traditional learning period is over… We can accept that a beginner crashes during their introduction if they are poorly supervised, but no one can see what could go wrong once that learning is over and the pilot has become seasoned. Thus, of all the superheroes with the power of flight (Superman, Wonder Woman, Peter Pan…), with flying devices (Iron Man, Harry Potter…), or even with the related power to defy gravity (Spider-Man, Batman…), none ever crashes once the initiation is over, even though many of them fall during this first learning phase. At a pinch, our imagination accepts a possible mechanical problem with the flying device as a cause of temporary failure, but no more. The reason is simple: since the superhero is perfect, they are not affected by human factor issues, and so they never have a flying accident. Those who fall are not superheroes, and everything comes together to make us believe that accidents are caused by human error (myth \#2) and that there is no limit to flight (myth \#4).

### A necessary rhetoric

Despite decades of massive, concrete safety efforts, combining many intentions and actors, accidents remain. The community still does not count them very well, but everyone knows it.

The first, natural justification put forward by our world is the “hothead” effect. It is true that in the early years, alongside the teaching effort deployed everywhere to teach, democratise and give a more reliable image of our activities, many beginners continued to teach themselves, alone or with their band of friends. This practice has still not disappeared, and there is no doubt that the probability of an accident with this kind of self-teaching is much higher than with more professional and supervised approaches. Many problems were therefore reported, and logically the myth of accidents “caused by human error” was strengthened.

- “The activity isn’t that dangerous and we teach it well, but these are real hotheads and they do any old thing.”

Nowadays, the word “hotheads” is increasingly replaced by “reckless”, but the argument remains the same. It is the one that helps turn the victim into a scapegoat.

Federation and commercial efforts to democratise these activities, together with the network of professionals developing across the country, with strong interests in training and in managing tourist flows, explain the link between the first two myths well. Barely caricaturing, the prevailing discourse then becomes: “Paragliding is great! Come and try it, anyone can learn, but to avoid accidents you have to get trained and/or be well supervised.” It was no doubt during the boom years (the 1990s) that this belief became most radical. Ten-year-old children were in the air, wings appeared in mass-market sports chains such as Decathlon, and big brands were considering investing in what might become a mass sport in the future.

The victims produced by our activities hold back a business that could be much bigger, and the collective and social pressure on those who have accidents does not diminish. Here I want to present yet another illustration of this victim/scapegoat ambivalence, which helps strengthen the power of our beliefs.

### The Icarus myth to the rescue!

If we go back to Greek mythology, Daedalus does tell his son Icarus to fly neither too low, so as not to weigh down his wings with the dampness of the sea, nor too high, so as not to melt the wings of feathers and wax he made for him. As everyone knows, the story ends badly because the son does not listen to his father. The moral is therefore that accidents happen through inexperience, failure to follow instructions, or pride. It is the consequence of human error, and it is the victim’s fault.

Unfortunately, there is something even more pernicious. In our ecosystem, every actor has a direct interest in blaming the pilot who has had an accident:

Fellow pilots want to keep believing that their sport is safe and want to keep flying. The wing manufacturer guarantees that its machine complies and wants to keep selling more. The instructor systematically clears their teaching and wants to keep teaching. The federation counts, analyses, raises awareness (at best) and wants to keep receiving members.

It is important to understand that the victim/scapegoat logic, however symbolically rooted, also has roots in the search for liability after an accident. It is more convenient, from every point of view, for the system and its actors to think that victims made mistakes and are now bearing the consequences. It is also easy to rationalise in hindsight, and it is a tendency that, for once, is found in full in civil and commercial aviation. In fact, no one can ever say, even once: “something other than the pilot failed!”

Thinking that Icarus is at fault means we do not ask Daedalus why he did not use a few silk threads if he knew the wax would melt. Why did he have his young son fly for so long, and over the sea, on the very first try? We see a kid losing his head and dying of his pride, but we forget to ask what could have been put in place to avoid the tragedy. We pass over in silence what went wrong, and we do not talk about what we should conclude from it for future flights.

Myth \#3, of a pilot who alone can ensure their own safety, comes from there. “Master on board, under God” is not so much a slogan to give the pilot full powers to carry out their mission. It is in fact what everyone wants. It implicitly makes the pilot responsible for any potential failure: if something goes wrong, it will be their fault. Most surprisingly, pilots themselves very often subscribe to this idea for its corollary, which implies that if everything goes well, it will be thanks to them.

### Our own success, other people’s mistakes

There is a fairly simple, almost mechanical explanation for our habit of blaming the victim. It lies in the way we attribute causes to what happens to us. In *Thinking in Bets*, former professional poker player Annie Duke describes it very well[^c-duke]. When things go well for us, we see it as the fruit of our skill. When things go well for others, we readily see it as luck. Conversely, our own failures are down to bad luck, while those of others are explained by their mistakes.

This mechanism has long been known in social psychology. Fritz Heider showed as early as 1958 that we constantly seek to explain behaviour, attributing it either to the person (their character, their skill) or to the situation (circumstances, chance)[^c-heider]. Several systematic biases have been grafted onto this attribution theory:

- **Self-serving bias.** We attribute our successes to our qualities and our failures to circumstances, which protects our self-esteem[^c-miller-ross].
- **The fundamental attribution error.** To explain other people’s behaviour, we overestimate the weight of their personality and choices and underestimate that of the situation[^c-ross].
- **The actor-observer asymmetry.** When we act, we mostly see the situation constraining us. When we observe, we mostly see the person acting[^c-jones-nisbett].
- **Belief in a just world.** We need to believe that everyone gets what they deserve, to the point of looking in the victim for whatever might justify what happened to them[^c-lerner].

Applied to free flight, these biases produce exactly what we have described in the previous pages. The pilot coming back from a difficult flight takes credit for having handled it well, when the air mass may simply have spared them. The pilot who has a scare talks of “rotten conditions”. But when someone else is injured or killed, we first look for what they did wrong: they were flying too close to the terrain, their wing was too demanding, they should not have taken off. That is what allows us to conclude: “it won’t happen to me”. Attribution is not just an individual reasoning error. It is the raw material of the scapegoat, and it feeds the myth of a pilot who is sole master of their safety.

This bias spares no one, not even our best pilots. Antoine Girard, distance world record holder, adventurer and a true hero of our community, says so with great frankness[^c-girard]:

> “In paragliding, we are the ones taking the risks. There is no random side, there’s no gust of wind that suddenly arrives. Those things don’t exist; there are very few accidents where we’re not responsible. I’m not saying I won’t die paragliding, because accidents can happen, but if I die, there’s a 98%, even 99% chance it’s because I made a mistake. And so if I die, it will be my fault. Whereas in the mountains… I’ve had enough friends who disappeared. There’s no mistake, everyone is there, and one person in the group disappears, sadly. A stone in the wrong place, anything, you never know when it’s going to fall.”

In another interview the same year, he comes back to what drew him away from high-level mountaineering[^c-girard-2]:

> “The notion of risk was very, very important to me, because in the mountaineering world, when you push it quite far, at a high level […], death is everywhere. […] I found it very, very unfair because it was Russian roulette, really: you’re walking, you don’t feel you’re taking any risk, nobody is taking any risk, and there’s a guy in front of you who disappears because he gets hit by a stone, an avalanche, something, while you, two minutes behind, are fine. And actually I don’t accept, or I really didn’t accept, that random risk-taking, whereas in paragliding… I’m not saying there are fewer accidents, all that… In paragliding, I think that everyone who has an accident, who gets killed or where there’s a problem, it’s 95, even 98% down to a human error at the start. We’re much less at the mercy of chance. If one day I die paragliding, it’ll be because I made a mistake, so I could have avoided it. I’ll have made a mistake, so that’s the consequence.”

The point here is not to criticise Antoine Girard. His words are those of an exceptional pilot, consistent with his flying and with the high standards he sets himself. That is precisely what makes them so revealing: at the summit of our discipline, the belief is at its peak. In the mountains, chance kills; in paragliding, it would always be the pilot. Accident reports tell a different story. In 2024 and 2025, in-flight collapses were the leading cause of reported paragliding deaths, and when they start close to the ground, the pilot often has time neither to recover the wing nor to throw the reserve (see “Knowing our accidents”). A gust that suddenly arrives does exist (see “Rejecting chance and uncertainty”, myth \#4). Attributing 98% of deaths to pilot error is exactly what attribution theory describes: a way of keeping control over what, in part, escapes us. Just because it is less visible than in mountaineering does not mean it does not exist. “A guy in front of you who disappears […] while you, two minutes behind, are fine”: that is exactly what also happens in paragliding, at times.


### The hero’s journey

Here I must deal with the submerged part of the iceberg and the reason why I think all the beliefs highlighted in the first part of this book do damage. In 1949, Joseph Campbell published a famous essay in comparative mythology: *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*. In it he posits the existence of a monomyth, a kind of structuring archetype for all the myths and folk tales of our societies. Every author and every era seems systematically to replay the same story, the same “hero’s journey”. Campbell describes the stages of this journey precisely.

*![](../images/voyage-du-heros.png)*
*Infographic (in French) – The stages of the hero’s journey, courtesy of Florent Salem https\://www\.florentsalem.fr/concept-le-monomythe-de-campbell*

Campbell’s analyses are much debated, but his journey has been taken up by many authors and by all of popular cinema. It is the spitting image of what a paraglider pilot experiences, on the scale of a career as on the scale of a single flight.

Leaving, being put to the test, coming back: that is what we live through every time we go flying. It is the hallmark of risk sports, because the outcome is uncertain. My hypothesis is not very pleasant to hear, but I think that by dint of seeing heroes, we wanted to become heroes[^21]. I believe risk sports are the symbol contemporary society uses to fantasise about its heroes[^22].

After years of consuming heroic films and stories, we paraglider pilots wanted to live the same story. To become the heroes of our own reality[^23]. Not necessarily out of narcissism. What we come looking for in the air is very varied: emancipation, knowledge, a culture of the air, contemplation, aesthetics, adrenaline, mastery, belonging, and yes, sometimes narcissism too. These motivations mix, and each of us nurtures our own, more or less consciously[^24].

I explained earlier how much the freedom of free flight is in fact that of transgression and of the relationship with limits. Being able to set oneself apart from most of one’s fellow humans, or from an earlier version of oneself. Coming back with this reward means completing a full journey cycle with a significant and uncertain trial. Either the traveller is indeed the chosen one, and will find along the way and within themselves the resources to pass the trial, or they are not, and so they will fail.

A potential trap appears as soon as this distinction is only made in comparison with a community of origin. Simply launching a paraglider is quite enough of a transgression for ordinary members of society, and yet it is no longer enough when the hero now belongs to a world where everyone already does it… The limit is pushed back, and with it comes the need for a new journey and a new transgression to regain the status sought within the community. The stars of our sport are those who have come through many journeys. The supreme event is the one in which the winner shows the whole world the extent of their power of transgression by shattering every limit imaginable: the X-Alps.

So it is as if, alongside the community’s prevailing discourse promoting sanitised, mass-market disciplines, pilots were in reality embarking on initiatory journeys and rituals far heavier with meaning. The justification and democratisation of our activities conceal what they fundamentally are: a wonderful creative space allowing citizens to find meaning again, to confront in nature and in practice the symbolism of the hero’s journey, ordeal included, and thereby to feel an infinite sense of freedom.

These rites and symbols may be widespread elsewhere in our society, but in risk sports they radically put physical integrity at stake. Paragliding victims recognise themselves as guilty of having failed a self-inflicted trial. The guilt here is that of the decision to leave and of the call to adventure (“I shouldn’t have gone”) or that of being unprepared: “I wasn’t in a fit state.” “I wasn’t ready.” “I was tired.” This transgressor’s syndrome signs and rationalises the hero’s failure. Those who have had an accident are left with two options: keep projecting themselves forward and set off on a journey again after a period of convalescence (the accident is then seen as one more trial, an obstacle on the path), or accept their humble condition and withdraw from the activity, forgetting the plan to go and seek their rewards.

I have something even harder to write. In this system, the death of our loved ones is unfortunately the proof that the ordeal was real (see “The freedom of free flight”). A trial from which everyone came back would not be a trial. It is our lost friends who give the journey its weight, and that is a terrible thing to say. They are, despite us, the hero’s ultimate justification. Some do not come back; I did. The mere fact of having come back underlines the power of what one has been through. Even when the only interpretation left to us is chance (“It’s a miracle I survived”), it gives oneself and the world the impression of being some kind of chosen one. The loved ones who are gone become, despite themselves, foils for those who remain. This is not a judgement; I am one of them. It is the story we tell ourselves that makes them play this role.

Once again, this mechanism is weaker in activities where death is perceived as a lottery. In mountaineering, as Antoine Girard said above, a stone falls on one person and spares the one walking two minutes behind: the survivor knows they were lucky. In paragliding, it is the opposite. Since we believe almost all accidents come from pilot error, the one who comes back owes nothing to chance. If they came back, it is because their skills did not fail. They are not lucky: they are all-powerful.

At the extreme, the community even has a word for these chosen ones: “SkyGods”[^c-skygod]. The expression alone says everything about the ordeal-based hierarchy among pilots. The SkyGod is the one who has passed the most trials, whose supposed skills allow them to face ever wilder skies. We watch them launch while the others stay on the ground, and we tell ourselves that they know. Yet statistics remind us that the gods of the sky are very humble mortals. The typical profile of a pilot reporting an accident drawn up by the FFVL is not that of a beginner, but that of “a male pilot over 40 who flies fairly regularly, an experienced rated pilot who has an accident in the afternoon on a known site, rather at the end of the flight”[^c-ffvl-profil]. And the mathematics of a pilot’s career (see “Who counts our dead?”) make no exception: the longer one flies, the more the risk accumulates. Yet SkyGods are precisely those who fly the most, the longest and in the strongest conditions. Very high-level pilots fall and die too; there are simply statistically fewer of them than ordinary pilots.

Those who come back tell of their journey, and those who do not come back tell nothing any more. This is where this mechanism meets the causal attribution discussed above. Statisticians call it survivorship bias: we draw lessons only from the cases that survived the selection and forget all the others. The pilot who came back naturally attributes their return to their skill, their judgement, their preparation. The share of luck disappears from their story, and with it the possibility of understanding what killed the others.

Crucially, let us note that what makes it possible to project oneself into this hero’s journey (as a paraglider pilot rather than in some other kind of adventure) comes precisely from our belief system. The combination of myths 1 and 2, of an activity conceived as safe but which must be tamed in order to survive it, allows a kind of “soft” ordeal in which modern pilots can engage with the feeling of taking a moderate risk that they will in fact be able to manage. That is how the community distances the activity from other forms of “Russian roulette” such as the mountaineering Antoine Girard talks about. Myth \#3, of the pilot as sole master of their destiny, is the very echo of the hero: it is they and no one else who must take charge to pass the trial and, if they come back, take all the credit. Myth \#4, in which no condition seems too strong for those who know how to overcome it, is part of the same imagination. Finally, the distortion of risk homeostasis theory serves to guarantee that if the hero has too much support, they will have to choose a harder quest to reach their goal.

The symbolism of the hero’s journey now reinforces the very beliefs that allowed it to take deep root in our imagination. It was not always so, however.

### How beliefs evolve

Our beliefs are built, evolve and strengthen over time and through the people who make up our community. It is interesting to see how successive generations of pilots and ideas about the activity shape each other. On the one hand, beliefs attract a particular type of participant, while at the same time these participants help to change the ideas slightly, allowing new, slightly different members to arrive, who in turn shape new beliefs. The direction of this development favours a broad democratisation of free flight as the belief in its reliability becomes established.

Contrary to what we intuitively think, early hang gliding and paragliding did not attract only daredevils and madmen. At first they in fact interested extremely polarised profiles. Very soon we find people focused on leisure, on gliding sensations and thrills, but further upstream and at the exact opposite, there were also people with proven technical skills, focused rather on understanding aerodynamic phenomena and mastering aircraft. Hippies, engineers. Inventors who were almost hippies and almost engineers too. Our activities would be nothing without the “Gyro Gearlooses” who took up the activity to build the necessary bridge between fun and open-air adrenaline on one side and the rigour and science that flight requires on the other. While these pioneers tried to make the aircraft a little more cooperative and efficient, the public drawn to this kind of activity was mainly of two types: either experienced, highly technical people from neighbouring disciplines or even from the military, who were aware of the risks and dangers inherent in these new disciplines; or people with very different professional skills but in full pursuit of thrills, as described by Marvin Zuckerman[^25].

As the first inventors worked to make the discipline safer and attitudes slowly changed under the impetus of early forms of institutionalisation, the public changed and grew with extremely skilled profiles. Often they were first instructors and practitioners of outdoor activities used to uncertainty and risk management. It was the time of mountain guides, ski instructors, climbers, pilots of other aircraft. At that time, you needed a certain pedigree to become a free-flying pilot, because the myth of a safe activity was still being built. It required either great skill or a good dose of ego, and some emblematic historical figures are famous for having mixed the two well.

This world stabilised during the 1990s, before paragliding caught on with teachers, primary school teachers and other knowledge professions. These were the first people of a more “general public” type, attracted by the possibility of mastering an aircraft in an aerial environment. Yet the activity was not yet designed for the “general public” as such, but teaching methods arrived and helped make the democratisation of paragliding thinkable as a new possibility.

There was great intellectual ferment; the network of professionals across the country organised itself and helped convert a larger part of the population, already won over and now convinced by the safety discourse and the apparent ease of paragliding. The system then took off, because seeing everyone do it reinforces the feeling of safety. This is when the activity opened up more widely to groups historically far removed from aerial disciplines: young people, women, people with disabilities. It was the era of PE teachers, school sport (UNSS), sports science degrees (STAPS) and university sports services (SUAPS). Only reality checks and serious accidents (of every profile) still partly held back the democratisation of our activities.

Today we find ourselves in this kind of reality check, with a paragliding that has taken many forms: speed riding, speed flying, ski-and-fly, parakiting, acro, freestyle, hike-and-fly, para-alpinism, soaring, competition or leisure, beginner vs. experienced, cross-country, accuracy landing, towing… Who wants what? The democratisation, then diversification and even individualisation (!) of outdoor physical activities have made them explode so much (Christian Pociello[^26]) that it becomes hard to believe that all these people are engaging in activities where risk management is in fact not very reliable. Our beliefs are also strengthened by this democratisation, whereas in reality our collective safety systems have not kept pace and are struggling to take shape. That is the paradox we are living. How does this democratisation still work today?

### How democratisation works

The types of free-flying pilots have diversified in step with the belief in the reliability of the aircraft. Our activity is passed on first of all through ideological proximity. The “bug” the beginner catches on getting “hooked” on flying is in fact a new confidence in a world they had never considered until then. The more an individual is immersed in the belief system of safe paragliding, the more likely they are to take it up in turn. Families get their children flying because they believe in an activity that can be learned and in a reasonable risk that can be controlled. The children of instructors or well-known figures in our community almost all fly from a very young age. Most parents do not even ask themselves the question. To a lesser extent, spouses, brothers and sisters, cousins… also often get started through family. This mode of transmission is extremely effective, since it is direct and intimate. Values are shared within the family or the immediate circle. The conversion/transmission rate is almost total.

Barely further from the world of flying, mountain communities and people very close to outdoor activities are also easily won over by paragliding, because it is easy to compare this discipline, in terms of risk, with the others available in their environment. Do more people die skiing, climbing, trail running, mountain biking or paragliding? It hardly matters. People readily see these activities as places where death generally comes to take the careless… In fact, the logic kept alive by the free-flying community is already there, present and underlying elsewhere. The transfer is easy. This channel of transmission is effective, with very strong parallels and connections between sports: para-alpinism, combo, ski-and-fly, hike-and-fly… This spread is fast.

Professional and club schools and social media take care of converting the rest. Paragliding figures show an activity essentially centred on a tourist consumer product. In our world, it is often said that fewer than 5% of introductory course trainees or tandem passengers go on to fly. I have found no published data to confirm this, and once again it is a figure no one measures. But no professional claims the proportion is high. People come for sensations, memories, a moment of adrenaline, nice photos for social media, a daring timeless image, on holiday… This route to democratisation draws a massive flow, which explains why, despite the small conversion rate we have mentioned, many pilots are converted every year through professional, club and tourist activity.

All these dynamics of democratisation of free flight sustain a steady rise in the membership of the French federation: 34,387 in 2023, 34,744 in 2024, 36,189 in 2025 and 37,680 in 2026[^c-ffvl-effectifs]. That is nearly 10% more in three years, and more than 4% a year over the last two years. At this rate, and even counting only federation members, the number of pilots in France would double in less than twenty years. Growth is not the same in every country in the world. You will notice that wherever free-flying communities are smallest, confidence in our favourite aircraft is not really shared or effectively spread socially. In many places, paragliding remains a matter for daredevils, as it was at the end of the 1980s in France.

In Europe, and especially around the Alps, the democratisation and undeniable appeal of paragliding were born from the spread of our own community myths. We have massively succeeded in convincing people that paragliding is an activity with controllable risk. Every day we reduce the perception of the risk inherent in flying our aircraft, while at the same time the heroic symbolism and the imagination of flight remain intact. As a result, people from backgrounds ever further removed from nature sports, risk management and aviation want to join us and fly. I am not criticising this movement, since I come directly from it; I am describing it. Nothing destined me to fly; my parents (especially my dad) believed it was safe because that is what they were told.
