## Myth \#1

Paragliding is not a very risky sport. You can fly in complete safety as long as you scrupulously respect a framework and know your own limits and your environment.[^c-mythe1-ex]

### An appealing idea

From an analytical point of view, this idea has several advantages. First, it makes each pilot responsible as an actor of their own flying and their own safety. It encourages pilots to know themselves, to train, to find out about their environment, to build up experience, and to fly within a framework and with equipment suited to their abilities. Adopting a personal risk management strategy and sticking to it would therefore let you control your destiny. Consequently, if everyone were really careful, the community could show an accident rate close to zero.

This view of paragliding as a sport whose risk can be controlled (or is in fact controlled) also conveniently makes it possible to democratise, insure, reassure and even sell our aerial activity on a very large scale.

- “If I do everything right, nothing will happen.”
- “With proper supervision, there’s no risk.”
- “Nah, I just take it easy.”


The FFVL’s *Manuel du vol libre* puts it with more nuance, but the same idea shows through: “Even with great experience, you are never safe from surprises. However, a sound knowledge of aerology makes it possible to avoid the most dangerous traps; after a good analysis of the conditions, only small, manageable surprises should remain…”[^c-manuel-surprises]. The danger is acknowledged, then immediately reduced to what a good analysis can control.

However deep-rooted and widespread it may be, this idea unfortunately does not survive observation of the real world. Pilots supervised by renowned and experienced instructors have accidents, and some of them sometimes die. We also see pilots disappear who never “push it”, as well as genuine stars of the sport. There is no typical profile of a pilot who dies. Paragliding is, unfortunately, not what free-flying pilots themselves say it is.

I am not the only one who thinks so. Christian “Chrigel” Maurer, the most successful pilot in the history of the Red Bull X-Alps[^c-xalps], places the origin of this illusion as early as flight school: “I think it starts quite early in the flight school. In a flight you get the feeling quite early paragliding is the safest sport in the world and it’s not true, because after 2 seconds, you’re quite high and it can be dangerous.”[^c-maurer] A little further on, he sums up: “you learn to fly with the wrong mindset”.

### Analysing the sporting activity

Looked at more closely, free flying is essentially:


- A desire for autonomy and to rise above our natural, earthbound environment.
  “Bye-bye, solid ground. I’m off to the sky!”

- A journey through an environment that is uncertain, or that generates uncertainty.
  When we say “It looks good” or “It’ll be fine”, it means it is not guaranteed. We think it will be fine, but forecasting and observation only tell part of the story.

- A game of vertigo and chance[^2]
  Pilots go into the air to play. Few pilots take to the air in order to respect a strict aeronautical, professional or commercial framework.

If we add an element of competition (Agon – racing against others) or of imitation (Mimicry – copying others) to this already very particular aerial environment, it is hard to imagine an activity where the risk of losing one’s life is completely absent.

What we actually do is move through a partly unreadable environment where vertigo, chance and commitment are inherent to the internal logic of the game. Paragliding is not a fundamentally “safe” activity, and being able to accept that and to fly anyway is an extraordinary freedom.

Having read Bastien Soulé several times[^3], I understand that the notion of a “risk sport” is fairly elastic. Taking stock, however, our sport meets the criteria for classification as a risk sport of every author who has undertaken the difficult task of categorising sports. There is no doubt that paragliding meets them all.

First, it is an activity in which the participant can die if they misjudge a situation, if their technique is inadequate for the conditions they meet, or if their equipment fails. Nicolas Penin speaks of “death being conceivable in the event of a technical or equipment error”[^4]. It is also an activity in which accidents remain frequent and whose consequences are sometimes the most severe possible, the two criteria used by Michel Bouet: “both maximum severity and high occurrence”[^5].

Next, paragliding can consume all of the pilot’s resources: their fatigue, concentration, endurance, and management of stress and emotions. This is one of the two forms of uncertainty described by Pierre Parlebas, the unknown of one’s own limits, from endurance to the exhaustion of one’s faculties[^6]. The other form is that of the environment: “it is the uncertainty of the environment, its wildness”, he writes. Our sky is full of it. There are SEVERAL weather models precisely because meteorology is not an exact science, and no aerological model at all, because we do not yet have the means to develop one…

Finally, as Jacques Defrance shows, it is an activity in which an accident is possible through confrontation with the environment and through unintentional destruction of the craft, that is, the loss of flight[^7].

### A hidden observation

Today these statements are neither clear enough to everyone nor written down plainly enough. They are basic observations that would entirely change the paradigm we are in if we kept them in mind.

- Perhaps, faced with such risks, we should give ourselves the means to organise safety collectively with a little more method? I will come back to this later.

- Perhaps it would be worth asking whether, and under what conditions, such an activity can really be opened up to everyone? Sold commercially? Is it for everyone? I am glad to have the freedom, the pleasure, the serenity and the strength to paraglide, and yet several times now I have held back from recommending it to friends or acquaintances. Encouraging such a decision is, after all, quite a responsibility!

Paragliding is not an activity whose risk can be controlled. Does such a sport even exist? The fundamental concept of a risk activity that one could control individually through a certain attitude and/or a high level of skill is a fundamental and singular belief of our free-flying community.

### A discordant and paradoxical public image

This myth also clashes with the public image we project outside our community. Our accidents are constantly reported in the local press; for each victim killed or seriously injured, the number of people affected is multiplied, and prefects are starting to take a very close interest in our accident statistics, given how much we use the rescue resources available in some regions (helicopters in particular). Even though the social acceptability of paragliding seems to improve over time, to the point of now winning over advertising space, it must not be thought of as acquired forever. The nationally known reference to a paraplegic paraglider pilot in the film *The Intouchables*[^8] perfectly illustrates this ambivalence, putting our favourite activity at the top of the box office while still underlining how dangerous it is. The fact is that, even within our own field, many people find our accident rate hard to bear. All this adds an undeniable social dimension that helps to classify paragliding as a high-risk sport.

Our accident record cannot be judged by us alone, members of the community who, literally and figuratively, generate our own statistics! Whether a risk is “controllable” or not, like whether a system is “reliable” or not, lies precisely in a subjective assessment or judgement. That judgement can come from inside the system: “paraglider pilots think their activity is safe and that the risks they take can be controlled”; but also from outside the system, in the manner of Rick Masters, who thinks our aircraft is stupid and doomed to fail. I coached a very high-level pilot who became an emergency medicine resident in Grenoble, and he told me he had changed the way he saw our activity after seeing so many badly hurt paraglider pilots come through. Are we the reasonable, responsible pilots we claim to be? Are we the slightly foolish surfers of the air that Rick Masters denounces? Are we simply unaware of the scale and impact of our passion? The gap between these inside and outside views of our activity is deep. So much so that I sometimes wonder how we still manage to keep up this discourse.
Here, then, is the logical next step and the relentless justification of this first belief.
