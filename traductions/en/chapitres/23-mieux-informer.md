## Informing better

Many of our accidents begin with information that did not get passed on. Sharing what we observe, in the air and on the ground, costs the pilot’s freedom almost nothing.

### A Beaufort scale for free flight

Speaking of being able to communicate effectively about a real danger in the air, it is curious that after 40 years of existence we still have no common scale to talk about the conditions met in the sky. How do I warn my colleagues on the ground about what I am experiencing in the air right now? How do I describe the conditions here when they are still one glide behind me?

Sailors have the Beaufort scale, which lets them estimate the state of the sea according to the wind. Thirteen increasing degrees from 0 to 12, from flat calm to full hurricane. We find it harder to model the element of air as well, because in our case the effects of wind are not always associated with turbulence. That should never have stopped us from building a common observation grid for sharing and prevention. We must be able to talk about the aerology we encounter. Competition pilots have developed a three-level system:

1) Good conditions.
2) Strong conditions or becoming threatening.
3) Conditions too strong or becoming too dangerous to fly.

Unfortunately, this system is not really applicable to wider use, recreational or professional, in which we look for more objective and descriptive (categorical) criteria. The three competition levels work (partly) among insiders sharing an implicit normative scale, monitored by an organisation that can decide to stop the task. For this reason, I propose a standardised descriptive system: WAT.

Wind / Ascent / Turbulence.
W: the maximum wind speed encountered, in kilometres per hour.
A: the maximum climb encountered, in metres per second, averaged over 20 seconds.
T: the maximum level of turbulence encountered, on a scale of 0 to 3.

| Level | Label | Description |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 0 | Stable – Laminar | No piloting needed in any circumstance. Stable pendulum. |
| 1 | Convective – Standard | Pitch control rarely needed. Some damping / braking. Moderate control inputs. |
| 2 | Turbulent | Active piloting. Thermal entries and exits must be piloted, disturbed airflow. Deep control inputs. |
| 3 | Severe | In-flight incident likely despite active piloting. Choppy air mass, including outside climbs. Reflex reactions to incipient incidents. |

This reference scale for the air is not perfect, and I urge the community to build a better one. It mixes objective observations with feelings and, obviously, a degree of personal skill. What is severe for one pilot may be merely turbulent for another. However, in the absence of any other framework, if tomorrow I hear a pilot announce a WAT of W30, A5, T3, it will help me stay on the ground. I know very well what that means, and I no longer want that at the moment.
In any case, it is a more precise indication than hearing “it’s a bit strong but it’s fine” or “there’s plus eight”, when the vario is still set to instantaneous. A famous anecdote on this subject: I remember a competition situation at Mévouillon, when I was a coach for the youth free-flight squad. The north wind is coming over the launch while we are launching facing south, and the meet director asks the poor souls going out low how the conditions are. One pilot answers: “It’s hammering to death.” Silence, then he goes on again: “TO DEATH!”, really insisting. And right after, he adds: “Level 2.” Just like that. It became an in-joke within the squad, because “Level 2” does not fit what came before at all. Announcing “Level 2” is supposed to indicate conditions becoming strong or dangerous, not a fight for survival.

### A personal experience scale in flight – the reference level

Unlike WAT, which aims to be a semi-objective framework for reading air conditions, it can be useful to share subjective feelings, put in context and related to one’s personal experience. On a scale of 0 to 10, 0 being the calmest flight and 10 the most terrible flight one has ever personally made, where am I right now?

Comparing this level with someone of roughly the same experience helps one think about how one feels about the conditions, and regulate one’s level of arousal, one’s morale or that of a nearby pilot. It is an indispensable coaching tool that I encourage people to use to detect over-arousal and the moments when pilots become discouraged and more vulnerable. Hearing a young pilot with 3 years of experience announce a reference level of 5 when you are yourself above 7 after 25 years of experience is never good, either way. For the record, this tool was born after one of my worst training sessions as a coach. I misjudged an aerological situation that deteriorated near Font-Romeu. After a good hour of fierce struggle to keep the youth squad in sight and try to show them a reasonably safe way out, I land with one of them in strong wind but away from trouble. He says to me:

- “That was a bit strong though, wasn’t it?”

To this day, that session is still my level 10. A horror. Where was he on his own scale? I would say 10 as well without hesitation, but supervision, youth and the rest meant he did not experience it that way. In such a case, it is important to go back over it and not to minimise, trivialise or derealise what happened, because there is a limit. And that day, it was close.

### A network of weather stations

We should also step up efforts on networks of weather stations. Overall, this is handled relatively well in France and around the Alps. The SPOTAIR and BURNAIR projects are remarkable examples in this respect. They are of paramount importance for safety, and we must cover flying areas generously with a fine mesh of stations. The aim is both to analyse aerological data after the fact and to warn of conditions in real time.

### Safety weather bulletins (BMS)

At sea, when the Météo-France forecast exceeds force 6 on the Beaufort scale, a safety weather bulletin (BMS) is posted in every harbour master’s office, signalled on every lighthouse and broadcast on VHF channel 16. It is hard to miss information about bad sailing conditions. The overwhelming majority of recreational sailors stay in port or at a sheltered anchorage. Those who put to sea are mainly professionals, accomplished sailors or well-informed sportspeople. We very often know about WAT conditions incompatible with free flight, but we do not talk about them much. Let us post bulletins in our schools and broadcast messages automatically by radio. Let us inform those we see heading up to launch when the radar already shows the thunderstorm and rain in the next valley. Every action in favour of sharing information fosters responsibility. We could start by relaying Météo-France’s orange and thunderstorm warnings.
