## The freedom of free flight

The first word that invariably comes up to describe what we feel when flying under a paraglider is “freedom”. It is all in the name: Free Flight. I will be honest: it took me a long time to understand the relationship between paragliding and freedom, and even longer to identify the workings of its dialectic with the notion of safety.

### Free flight and emancipation

Flying is a varied form of emancipation. First of all, of course, it is an escape from gravity, the permanent and mysterious force that pins us to the ground. A power, a possibility to rise, to explore or to reach a new space with greater ease and against nothing but the resistance of the air.

A liberation from the troubles of the Earth, too. In Greek mythology, Icarus flees the labyrinth by air. The wings of feathers and wax made by his father, Daedalus, allow him to escape the island of Crete and the tyrant king Minos, just as we often escape a reality that is a little too heavy on the ground. The third dimension, altitude and the sky are sources of freedom in that they offer an alternative environment to the turmoil of human society. Flight offers the fleeting freedom of hanging above the space and time of earthbound people.

Those who fly then gain a different perspective on the world, literally and figuratively. Flying is more than the mere aesthetics of a landscape left below or the aerial reconnaissance of a two-dimensional territory. It is an opening onto the understanding of natural phenomena and the knowledge of one’s own environment. The height Icarus reaches is the depth of the science and teaching of his genius of a father. It is also the trust needed in the world and in the laws of physics that govern it. Finally, it is the strength to believe in oneself and the boldness inseparable from any take-off. This relationship to knowledge is another emancipating force, culturally, mentally and even spiritually.

Beyond the various forms of emancipation described above, our discipline’s promise of freedom also lies in the notion of accessibility.

### Free flight and accessibility

Technically, legally and economically, our fabric machines are the ones that today provide the easiest and quickest access to the air. Being able to treat oneself to the sky without spending years in training to get there is a form of freedom. More democratic than aviation, easier than other ultralight gliders, and practically unregulated in most parts of the world.

Free flight is thus an accessible aerial activity practised not on airfields but in the natural environment. In the 1980s, paragliding and hang gliding no doubt emerged as hybrid activities at the crossroads of aviation, board sports and what were then commonly called outdoor nature sports (APPN). Enough to attract a larger share of the population in search of leisure, new spaces and new challenges. Here, freedom is to be understood as the autonomy offered to pilots. Autonomy from infrastructure and institutions, but also from the engine as a means of propulsion, from aeroplanes and other machines.

### Free flight and release

Historically, free flight is also the opposite of captive flight. Here, freedom is to be understood as release, marking the difference from parasailing and other towed devices. Doing away with the cable finally makes us free as a bird.

### Free flight and ultimate freedom

We have already looked at freedom through the prism of emancipation, accessibility, autonomy and release, but I now want to stress an even more fundamental facet offered by our discipline. We almost never talk about it, and I believe it is mainly what pilots describe when they say they feel “free” under their wing. Perhaps it comes neither from the joy of having left a few troubles on the ground, nor from the raw satisfaction of simply being able to reach the sky, nor from the relative freedom of movement in three dimensions that our aircraft allow. Looking more closely, we cannot really do whatever we want or go wherever we please on a paraglider. There are constraints in terms of equipment, time, space and environment. We have to accept a glide ratio predetermined by the laws of physics, fly within a limited speed range, bound by the daylight hours… We are in fact limited on all sides by air-mass conditions that push us to adopt and adapt procedures (taking off and landing into wind) and to follow a rough kind of flight plan. We must also take care to avoid certain airspaces that are literally forbidden or regulated for us. The sky is not, in substance, the symbolic freedom we make of it in our romantic or poetic imaginings. On the other hand, visiting it allows a more radical and deeper taking of freedom than any of those we have mentioned so far.

While we all know how our activity regularly takes lives or causes its share of accidents, we enjoy the ultimate freedom of engaging in it anyway. In a society whose flagship values revolve precisely around life, longevity, the pursuit of happiness, youth and health, and where everything, every day, tends to become a little more regulated and sanitised, when do we take this kind of risk? Sigmund Freud wrote[^13]: “Life is impoverished, it loses in interest, when the highest stake in the game of living, life itself, may not be risked.”
Who, along with paraglider pilots, would expose themselves at will to such situations of life-threatening uncertainty? A few road delinquents with their night-time joyriding, a few big-time criminals between drug runs and settling of scores, a few mercenaries, a few drug addicts… Villains, or else heroes too: mountaineers, firefighters, rescuers, extreme sports athletes, police officers, soldiers, divers… All of them share immediate access to a special status in the imagination of the general public and of our societies. Eccentrics, brave souls, lost souls, exceptions, nonconformists, all free and released from a certain kind of fear. To fly, you have to thirst for this freedom, which goes hand in hand with choosing to take the risk of dying.

To paraglide is, consciously or not, to enter one of the four fundamental elements (air, earth, water, fire) in order to undergo a kind of trial there. Trying to survive it and come back gives us a form of reassurance and justification. In psychoanalysis as in ethnology, we recognise here beyond any doubt the very definition of an ordeal-like practice. An ordeal is a ritual in which the participant submits to a form of higher judgement. “As if risking the highest stake placed the subject above every rule and every convention,” writes Marc Valleur[^14].

The ultimate freedom of paragliding lies in its ability to offer us a journey through the air in which we cross a line beyond which many of our fellow humans dare not venture. It is a form of transgression in which everyone knows that the ultimate risk is potentially to their life. That is what gives us this famous “feeling of freedom”. Having transgressed and risking a great deal, everything is suddenly more relative. It is in this context that regulation appears at best as futile, but more often as a hindrance or a form of enslavement:

- Futile, because transgressors do not care about rules. They go beyond them to undergo more personal and deeper trials. The judgement is intimate; it most often comes from one’s inner self or, more rarely, from some form of spirituality. In any case, human laws only matter if one can come back from the trial, and that is why they are secondary.

- A hindrance, because if the activity were in fact completely sanitised and safe, it would instantly cease to represent this ultimate form of freedom. Without transgression and without uncertainty, the original spirit fades. The feeling of being free disappears. No airline pilot boasts of the “freedom” offered by their aircraft.

- Enslavement, since the relationship with death is something each person grasps individually. Collective moderation of risk is viewed with suspicion, since everyone wants to be free to perceive risk and its different degrees however they see fit… What looks like madness to one person may be perfectly normal and safe to another. We are close here to the notion of free will, and I would like to share an anecdote about it.

One night, as I was walking along a sea wall that was off-limits at high tide, with a rough ocean, I was knocked off balance by a couple of very salty breakers. As I beat a retreat, not without difficulty, a lady older than me came to lecture me:

“Really, are you out of your mind or what? What are you playing at? You can see it’s forbidden!”

I can still hear myself replying spontaneously:

“I’ll die where I like.”

With great wit, she said:

“Not on my patch!”

As we can see, pilots react very strongly to attempts to moderate their risk-taking. That is because they feel one of their fundamental rights is under attack. Every attempt at safety adjustments or collective agreements that would touch the emancipation, accessibility, release and ultimate freedom of free flight is unbearable, because it symbolises the bird being put in a cage. Free-flying pilots overwhelmingly claim the right to take responsibility themselves, without outside moderation, for their commitment and their risk-taking. They accept prevention messages and training as long as these do not threaten their free will and their ability to engage in their activity in their own way. On the other hand, pilots are wary of forms of collective safety management and of regulations that might, they think, reduce their fundamental freedom to fly or force them to change their individual way of flying.

This opposition between individual freedom and a form of safety defined and organised collectively is reinforced by another major myth of our community.
