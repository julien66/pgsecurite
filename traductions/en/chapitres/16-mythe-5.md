## Myth \#5

Risk homeostasis theory makes it impossible to reduce accidents through external measures, since it assumes that pilots’ target level of risk will always remain unchanged, regardless of the environment.

### A misread theoretical endorsement

This myth serves as a defence and theoretical endorsement of the established paradigm and of the combination of the first three founding myths. Since having external constraints imposed to try to reduce accidents would be a big problem, risk homeostasis theory is invoked indiscriminately.

This rejection of constraints rests on a distinction that keeps coming up in our community: that between active safety and passive safety. The first refers to what the pilot does to avoid an accident: their preparation, their decisions, their piloting. The second refers to what protects them when an accident happens anyway: a more forgiving wing, a back protector, a reserve parachute, a well-designed site, fast rescue. In our discourse, the hierarchy is fixed in advance: active safety is noble, passive safety almost suspect, as if it invited negligence. The federation programme “Voler mieux” (Flying better) thus aims to help pilots progress “in terms of active safety”[^c-voler-mieux]. And the “active pilot” is constantly held up as an example, as if there were such a thing as passive piloting![^c-active-flying] Setting the two against each other once again puts everything on the pilot’s shoulders. High-risk industries do the opposite: they start from the principle that humans will one day get it wrong, and they build protections around them that do not depend on their vigilance. Active and passive safety are not competitors; they add up.

Our reference manual gives a striking example. Without ever citing Wilde, the FFVL’s *Manuel du vol libre* presents a “surprising yet so logical little theory”: the accident level of risk sports is supposedly “very similar from one sport to another”, because with “too many accidents” participants reduce their risk-taking and with “not enough” they take more. The conclusion it draws is not collective but individual: “The statistics exist and are consistent with the theory above. It’s up to you not to be part of them!” And the paragraph ends: “It is not the risk attributed to a sport that makes it dangerous, but the level of vigilance of the person practising it”[^c-manuel-homeostasie]. The manual does not say which statistics it means, and cites none. A theory about the levers a society can act on is thus reduced to an injunction addressed to each individual: be vigilant, and the accident will fall on others.

We must make the effort to reread Gerald J. S. Wilde[^16]. Unambiguously, the basic premise is that participants will adapt their activity to match their perception of risk to a “target” risk (often also called “preferred” risk in the literature), which is purely subjective and which seems acceptable or even desirable to them. That is how we all noticed in PE lessons that the thicker the mats, the more we wanted to jump hard and attempt the impossible, since we felt better protected on landing. Our perception being: “Since the mat is bigger, I can let go and have fun; I won’t get hurt any more.”

### A few corrections

I think it is important to set out three observations useful to our sport:

First, this means that anything outside the field of the “perceptible”, in the sense of direct (sensory) perception and of the meaning the participant gives it in action, does not affect the calculation and regulation of behaviour in the face of risk. Having a satellite positioning device in your harness pocket has no effect on the perception of risk in paragliding. No one feels or sees themselves as better equipped against risk with such a device. In plain terms, you do not feel, and you do not tell yourself, that you can escape an accident more easily with this box (unlike, for example, a reserve parachute or a more stable wing).

Second, as long as the perceived level of risk is indeed at its maximum (read: “I could die here”), all marginal adjustment measures (a helmet, a better back protector…) lead to marginal adjustments in participants’ behaviour. When you have clearly in mind the damage a cliff impact can cause, no one feels safer with a helmet, and no one feels their preferred risk seriously threatened by such passive safety equipment.

Above all, and if only one point were to be remembered, we must come to understand that the perception of risk in the moment is not the only factor that determines the participant’s adjustments. There is a cost/benefit balance that determines the very basis of the target risk, that is, the one preferred and used by the participant! This is box 1 of Wilde’s diagram. Four factors influence this initial definition of the target risk (1 and 4 being the most important according to the author):

1) The benefits of risky behaviour
2) The costs of risky behaviour
3) The benefits of safe behaviour
4) The costs of safe behaviour

And so, when the PE teacher announces four hours of detention for anyone who does a somersault, even with 60 cm thick mats, the target (newly “preferred”) risk of the pupils in a class drops dramatically. This is point 2 (“Costs of risky behaviour”); it is the speed camera policy on the roads, and in fact, theoretically, even in the light of risk homeostasis theory, it works! Note, however, that according to the author it would be preferable to act on points 1 and 4: reducing both the benefits of risky behaviour and the costs of safe behaviour.

In short, risk homeostasis theory does not say it is impossible to lower participants’ target or preferred risk through external constraints. It says exactly the opposite. It says that participants constantly adjust the risk they perceive to a subjective target risk, which is itself conditioned by a cost/benefit balance.

### Wilde, help!

At present, in the light of this theory, free-flying pilots have problems at every level. On the one hand, perceived risk is cheerfully played down by all the beliefs of our community. We have seen that the idea of a safe activity with “controllable” risk has spread widely and that, in this sense, paragliding is often perceived as a classic outdoor activity, accessible to all.

On the other hand, even though our perceived risk is already low, we have bad signals in each of the four factors that would allow us to lower our target risk.

1) The benefits of risky behaviour
- Image of a superhuman or prodigy. Social media. Overvaluation.
- Maximum performance offered by the most radical equipment.


2) The costs of risky behaviour
- Widespread idea of controllable risk. “It’ll go.”
- A democratised, outdoor-style sport. Everyone does it. “It’s easy.”
- A forgiving activity. Even young people do it. Towards a sport for all?
- Light regulation. Almost no disciplinary procedures.


3) The benefits of safe behaviour
- Literally to be invented. Even insurance is the same for everyone!


4) The costs of safe behaviour
- Loser image. No one inspires dreams by giving up.
- Minimum performance with safer equipment.
- Long and expensive training, not compulsory and with nothing in return.

When perceived risk is low and, at the same time, preferred risk is high, behaviour tends to become more extreme. We must both reset our perception of risk (paragliding is not an activity like any other) AND create a new cost/benefit balance to change our tendency to aim for a high target risk.
