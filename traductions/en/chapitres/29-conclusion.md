# Conclusion

Paragliding is not a safe sport with controllable risk. It is a magnificent activity in which you can die, and that is precisely why it makes us so free. As long as we refuse to say so, we will go on putting the blame for our accidents on their victims, and we will go on burying our friends while telling ourselves they made a mistake.

### What we believe

I have tried to show where our beliefs come from and why they hold so well. They fit into five sentences that each of us has already said or heard:

- “Paragliding isn’t dangerous if you respect a framework and know your limits.” (myth \#1)
- “Accidents are caused by human error.” (myth \#2)
- “The only one who can ensure my safety is me.” (myth \#3)
- “There are no conditions that are too strong, only pilots who aren’t good enough.” (myth \#4)
- “Anyway, if you protect pilots, they’ll just take more risks.” (myth \#5)

These beliefs protected an emerging activity, made it credible and helped it grow. They are no longer enough. There are more of us, our types of flying are more diverse, and our accidents now affect every profile, including the most experienced and the most closely supervised. These beliefs also have a hidden cost: they turn our victims into scapegoats, they divert the best safety tools (Reason, human factors, Wilde) so that nothing changes, and they prevent us from counting our dead.

### What we could do

The choice is before us. We can stay small and carry on as before. Or we can grow big: say honestly what we do, welcome our accidents, collectively build the protections we lack, and distinguish commercial leisure flying from personal commitment.

The proposals in the second part do not rest on a single pair of shoulders. That is their whole point: safety is a collective matter. To finish, here is who could do what.

| Who | What could change |
| :---- | :---- |
| The federation and international bodies | Count and publish our accidents anonymously, open a shared database and a lessons-learned system, publish a list of our dead, promote just culture, propose a charter for equipment and training by type of flying, defend an emergency radio frequency and protocol, support a victim support association. |
| Schools and professionals | Clearly distinguish tourist flying from pilot training, inform passengers and trainees honestly, value real progression courses, guide each student towards a mentor, run debriefings without looking for a culprit. |
| Clubs and site managers | Map accidents on their sites, equip and signpost fields (including the risk of water landings), install weather stations, post safety bulletins, organise watch and volunteer rescue in the major flying areas. |
| Manufacturers and laboratories | Flag uncertified sizes and models, publish readable test reports, report manufacturing defects, innovate on the reserve parachute and on protection close to the ground. |
| Mentors and coaches | Follow pilots over time, help them choose their quest, their equipment and their training modules, and remain a disinterested voice. |
| Every pilot | Train for their type of flying, sign the charter, fly with equipment suited to their type of flying, look out for others, report their accidents and incidents, and tell what happened to them without shame. |

None of this requires piling on regulation. Most of these measures will even make us freer, because we will know that if the worst happens, someone will look for us, find us and learn from what happened to us.

### For those who have fallen

I have had to attend too many funerals. I hear the same refrains there: “He died doing what he loved, from his passion.” “Dying paragliding is a beautiful death.” “Anyway, he wasn’t going to end up in a care home.” Beyond our need for consolation, these phrases above all underline and support the choice pilots themselves made to take up our activity. “She knew perfectly well what she was doing.”

Except that our lost friends were most often in full health, in the middle of their lives, with loved ones, plans and many more flights to make. They were not people at the end of their lives or in a state of dependency, waiting for some form of release. I refuse to associate paragliding with death, whether as fate or as some kind of romantic beauty. Dying of one’s passion, as if being able to live it were not enough, no longer speaks to me at all.

I began this book with first names. I end it thinking of them. I do not believe our friends died because they were less good than us. I believe they died in an activity that involves perils, within a community that has not yet built everything it could to protect them. The best tribute we can pay them is to learn from what happened to them and to make sure the same thing does not happen to others.

We will go on flying at our own risk. Let us make sure we no longer face our perils alone.
