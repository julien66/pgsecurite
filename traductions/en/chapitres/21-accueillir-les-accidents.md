## Welcoming accidents. Recognising victims

### A new culture of transparency and responsibility

Let us start with this. Accidents happen, and we must welcome them. With transparency and responsibility. This work is barely done at all, except through the narrow lens of accident reports and insurance. We would gain by caring for victims and their families more comprehensively and with new values. Of course, some tragedies are and will be hard to bear; some will again be particularly controversial and will directly involve various liabilities. So be it. That is the current state of what our community and our society produce. Let us accept it.
Let us at least have the courage to say factually how our fellow pilots fall or have fallen. In all circumstances, even if we have to do so with conflicting points of view. Let us stop putting everything on the backs of the pilots who are gone and listing only their mistakes. Today, every actor behaves as if their own liability might be engaged, and the natural reflex is a code of silence. At best, federations note the circumstances, pass them on to the insurer and count them. In the most developed ones, perhaps someone even conducts an internal investigation. A few now publish a list of deaths or an annual analysis (see “Who counts our dead?”), but almost none publishes its investigations or speaks publicly about an accident. This is a curious defence mechanism, because it is an outdated strategy anyway…
This is the age of social media and of the (sometimes hasty) search for liability and compensation. Silence from institutions or professionals is perceived at best as amateurism and at worst as an attempt to cover things up. Keeping quiet very often adds fuel to the fire. The courts have already heavily convicted large institutions, including the FFVL, as well as organisers and professionals, in France and in various countries. Today, cases are under way all over the world. Some may or may not lead to new convictions. There is no example where silence has improved things. When there is a proven problem or a presumed fault, insurers, beneficiaries, victims, prosecutors, the whole ecosystem tenses up, and there is no point in trying to get off lightly. Acknowledging wrongs and looking for deep, systemic causes without hiding behind the victim’s failings are the right attitudes; they move the whole community forward and are also more effective strategies in terms of defence and law. In many cases, the institution makes a better culprit and is more solvent than an instructor or a meet director. Yet any failings must be expressed transparently, and volunteers, professionals and other pilots must not be left alone facing liabilities that are in fact easily shared.

### Promoting just culture

Just culture was born in aviation and high-risk industries. James Reason described it as early as 1997[^c-reason97], and Sidney Dekker made it the subject of a reference book[^c-dekker-jc]. It rests on a simple idea: you cannot learn from mistakes if those who make them are afraid to talk about them.

A just culture is not a culture of impunity. It distinguishes the honest error, which every human makes and which must not be punished, from deliberately dangerous behaviour or gross negligence, which remain reprehensible. In between, it seeks to understand rather than to punish.

Civil aviation has drawn concrete schemes from it. In the United States, NASA’s ASRS has been collecting confidential incident reports voluntarily submitted by pilots since 1976[^c-asrs]. In Europe, Regulation (EU) No 376/2014 organises the collection of safety occurrences and protects those who report them. Airline pilots report their mistakes because they know they will not be punished for them[^c-ue376].

With us, it is the opposite. We have seen how the victim becomes a scapegoat (myth \#2). Anyone who tells their incident exposes themselves to the judgement of their friends, to comments on social media and sometimes to legal or insurance consequences. It is therefore rational to keep quiet. To go back to the Bradley curve (myth \#3), a just culture is the necessary step towards the interdependent stage: we cannot look out for each other if we do not dare tell each other what happened to us.

Promoting just culture in free flight would mean:

- Welcoming every account of an accident or incident without judgement, starting with the question “how did it happen?” rather than “what did you miss?”
- Guaranteeing the anonymity and confidentiality of reports, clearly separating them from insurance and legal procedures.
- Writing down, in black and white, in federations, clubs and schools, the line between honest error and fault.
- Publicly thanking those who share their experience, as “One turn, throw the reserve” managed to do for the reserve parachute.
- Training instructors, officials and organisers to run debriefings without looking for a culprit.

#### Publishing one’s own history

I am asking our community to tell its accidents. It would be dishonest not to start with mine. Few people suspect it, but my list is long. Here it is, warts and all.

- **2001, Cotze (Puigmal).** About twenty flights under my belt, on an Area Aspect (DHV 2) too big for me. I discover autorotation. *What the system could have done:* no one checked that a beginner fresh out of school was flying a wing suited to their level and weight… Not to mention the choice of site!
- **2001, same site.** On a small Colibri, I get blown backwards in a ridge venturi and end up in the fir trees, perched about ten metres up. My friends come to rescue me. *What the system could have done:* pass on the site’s aerology, and this venturi in particular, to those starting out there. Cotze in a westerly is tricky even for an experienced pilot.
- **2002, La Seu d’Urgell.** On an ITV school wing, still going backwards, I end up in the apple trees. Almost a kilometre behind the official landing field. *What the system could have done:* notice it was the second time, and help me understand why.
- **2004, two years of flying, SIV course.** On a Windtech Quarx XXS prototype, I do my first full stall, and I do not really understand when to raise my hands. I go through 300 m of cascade, the wing almost stabilised, sometimes flying backwards, sometimes in deep stall. *What the system could have done:* question a prototype on an SIV course in the hands of a pilot with two or three years of flying.
- **2005, Moulis (Ariège).** I botch a launch on lovely green grass soaked by light rain. My brand-new Supair Cocoon harness acts like a sledge and carries me to the break in slope, where I tip over into free fall down the cliff. Not a scratch, because my Edel Excel, which followed me balled up into the void, catches in the trees below. I am 1 m off the ground, shaking; it was a close call. *What the system could have done:* no training in the rain. Not even to “just fly down”. Teach me to launch with a pod harness before I discovered it in a real situation.
- **2005, same equipment.** An end-of-day soaring session, messing around close to the ground, classic overconfidence: a turn that does not clear the terrain. A stable compression fracture of the first thoracic vertebra. *What the system could have done:* nothing more than it did. This one is on me. But no form would say what led me to feel so sure of myself after so many warnings.
- **2008, my first World Cup.** I did all right on the first task and I want to fly back to the hotel. Above the town, I can no longer glide. I find a tiny landing spot, a small field with a horse in the middle. It is tight, but I think it will move out of the way. It is tied on a short chain, but I cannot see that yet. Loaded like a cement mixer, half wingover-ing so as not to miss the field, I hit its flank before slamming into the ground. It rears up like Tornado in *Zorro*, breaks its chain and injures its legs breaking my lines to free itself. I get dragged behind it for a while: para-joring. No harm to me, very sad for the poor horse. Charles Cazaux, today a colleague at the FFVL and a friend, repairs my wing all night. *What the system could have done:* nothing governed this flight outside the task, nor the euphoria of a good result.
- **22 July 2011, Bayramören World Cup (Turkey).** I am flying an UP two-line prototype that I know and like. I am exhausted from the journey, and we have just lost Xavier Murillo. I launch with music in my ears to cheer myself up and I do not feel the frontal collapse coming as I leave launch. I misjudge my height, I do not dare put the speed back on for fear of hitting too hard. I do not think I have time to throw, and I stay half in deep stall until I crash heavily: five more vertebrae. Jean-Marc Malhonda scrapes me up with the rescue team. Thank you again! *What the system could have done:* it had already done so, halfway. After the double fatal accident at the Piedrahita World Championships, the CIVL had already suspended competition class wings in its championships. The PWCA did not yet apply this: it only restricted the World Cup to certified wings from 2012[^c-fai-ccc]. For me, it came too late.
- **2013, trainee tandem instructor.** There is 30 km/h on the weather station; that is common on the site and for the school I work for, but I am apprehensive and my technique is perhaps still too rough. On top of that, my passenger falls during the inflation. I kill the wing but we are dragged at full power for a few metres (maybe 5), nothing serious for me, except that the ground is full of stones and my poor passenger has really nasty grazes on her legs. I take her down to a medical centre… very embarrassed and very ashamed. A minor outcome, but I hurt someone. Sorry again. *What the system could have done:* not leave a trainee instructor alone on a tandem with 30 km/h on the weather station, just because it is “common on the site”.
- **2014, regional youth training squad, new coach, on a Peak 3.** Training in Cerdanya, the forecast good weather deteriorates and we find ourselves in the middle of a huge valley convergence, under really cold and unstable air. To the east, a damp sea of cloud pushed by a 40 km/h sea breeze prevents us from landing. To the west, the wind is picking up just as much and we are making very slow progress. As a group, with under-age pilots, we climb at 8 m/s in a straight line, into wind, until we disappear into the cumulus congestus. I lose visibility and decide that is not acceptable for a coach: I must be able to see. I come down by stalling for a long time, twice. It does not work, because each time I end up back in the lift and then in the cloud. We finally escape under my radio instructions by pushing west, after a few scares and far too long. Everyone is safe. *What the system could have done:* collect this account. With no one injured, this major incident exists nowhere.
- **2015, Millau, back to competition with the youth squad.** First official task since my accident in 2011, on a fine, well-honed blue Boomerang 11, I get a massive frontal collapse just after the start. The wing disappears behind me, and everything happens so fast that when it comes back, my foot is still on the speed bar. A big surge, three twists straight after, and it starts to spin. Luckily, I manage to pull hard on the brakes, which are not completely jammed, and stall the wing. Another pilot shouts at me on the radio, several times, to throw my reserve. I estimate my height, undo the twists one by one, then release the wing and resume flight. A good 300–400 m of mess. *What the system could have done:* here again, collect the incident. It is in no database. It says something about racing wings, even in 2015.
- **2026, Superfinal.** After turnpoint B1, I hit the wing of a Brazilian friend. He is flying below my feet, in front of me, and lets off the speed bar just as I want to accelerate. I do not see him until he shoots up into my field of vision. I let go of everything, I hang on the B-risers, too late, contact: I fly into his line cone and tear out some of his lines on the way through. It clears by itself because he falls. He recovers, as he knows how to, then carries on. On my side, with my harness zip open, I try to finish the 100-plus km of the task with the others in boiling conditions. It feels much worse, I gradually fall behind and end up alone. The day ends with a huge frontal collapse at the convergence on final glide. With a cravat, I want to play it safe and deal with the problem using the stabilo line, without fancy stalling at the end of a task. To get more range, I let go of the brake, which is in my way. It ties itself in knots all around the stabilo. I finally clear the cravat by doing a B-stall, with no right brake. Rambo mode. I am almost surprised it works, but the brake is still wrapped around the stabilo, useless. I decide that is enough, I abandon the task and make an emergency landing on the B-risers among the olive trees. *What the system could have done:* collect near misses and collisions without injury, which say everything about density in competition.

Twelve episodes, including two spinal fractures. How many appear in a database? Two: the fractures, the only accidents requiring an insurance report. And for those that are recorded, what does the analysis form retain? “Piloting error”, “overconfidence”, “unsuitable equipment”. All true. But nothing that taught me anything is analysed or recorded.

This is just one example of a pilot’s path. I have not mentioned everything, because it is colourful enough already. You have the gist. Other pilots will obviously have less extraordinary stories. Yet I know very prominent figures in our world who hide theirs, and I respect that: most of the time, this kind of confession ends up being used against you. That is precisely the problem.

I am not telling all this to be pitied, nor to be judged. Some will judge me anyway, and that reflex is exactly the one this book describes. I am publishing my history because I am still here. Between the recognised coach and pilot I have become and the pilot who died young that I could have been, there is still luck. What we readily attribute to our skill owes a great deal to chance (see “Our own success, other people’s mistakes”). Not only is that where I come from, it is also what I have seen. Many pilots of the French team have been lucky and did not die in situations where they could have.

Let us publish our histories. Stars and ordinary pilots alike. Let us lance the boil and help the whole community shift its gaze. First, I have no reason to be ashamed of having crashed. Second, the community surely has a lot to learn from what happened to me. #ICRASHEDTOO

### Listening to uncomfortable voices

A just culture is not limited to welcoming those who have had an accident. It also means welcoming those who say what we do not want to hear. On this point, our community has a worrying reflex: it banishes non-consensual views. Rick Masters is the clearest example. His messages were received as provocations, the man rejected (see “Who counts our dead?”), and his old website is no longer online. Yet part of what he said was right, starting with that zone close to the ground where a collapse is unforgiving.

Annie Duke devotes a key passage of *Thinking in Bets* to this question[^c-duke-groupes]. To make better decisions, she explains, you need to surround yourself with a group whose common goal is to seek the truth rather than to reassure itself. Such a group thrives on disagreement. It values the minority opinion, because it is often the one that sees what the others do not want to see. It goes as far as organising dissent, with “devil’s advocates” or teams tasked with taking apart the group’s conclusions. The comfort of consensus, by contrast, is the ideal breeding ground for the biases we described in the first part.

Duke draws on the norms that sociologist Robert K. Merton attributed to the scientific community as early as 1942, summed up by the acronym CUDOS[^c-merton]:

- **Communalism**: data and knowledge belong to everyone and must be shared.
- **Universalism**: the value of an idea does not depend on who puts it forward.
- **Disinterestedness**: we seek the truth, not to be right or to defend our interests.
- **Organised scepticism**: every claim must be open to testing, including the most widely accepted.

Applied to free flight, these four norms have very concrete consequences. Communalism means opening up accident data. Universalism means accepting that a fair criticism remains fair, even when it comes from an American hang glider pilot we find unpleasant. Disinterestedness means recognising that manufacturers, schools and federations have interests, and that they cannot be the sole judges of the safety of our activities. Finally, organised scepticism means accepting that our most firmly established certainties, such as those this book calls myths, should be regularly put to the test.

Listening is not approving. One can reject Rick Masters’ conclusions about paragliding and keep his figures and his warnings. What a truth-seeking community cannot do is silence the person who disturbs it instead of checking what they say. In practical terms, our bodies could invite critical voices onto their safety committees, publish the counter-analyses they receive, and appoint, in every working group, someone whose role is to disagree.

### What our forms cannot record

A system only sees what it knows how to record. So let us look closely at our tools.

The FFVL accident report form offers two sections of contributing factors. The first concerns the pilot. It offers a closed list of fourteen boxes: poor observation, poor control, inattention, poor choice of take-off or landing, poor judgement of distances, poor assessment of the wind, impaired judgement, overconfidence, over-piloting, poor pre-flight check, inexperience, poor assessment of the weather, poor understanding of safety rules, and heavy traffic. Thirteen of these fourteen boxes describe a fault of the pilot. The second section concerns equipment and is limited to four boxes: the wing, the harness, the reserve parachute and the winch. That is all. There is no box for the site, the terrain, an unmarked obstacle, incomplete mapping, the organisation of a competition or a course, radio communication, rescue, or regulation.

The 2024 and 2025 reports show it. Out of 1,449 reports, 611 record at least one pilot-related factor, and only 47 an equipment-related factor[^c-ffvl-grille]. The other factors have nowhere to be recorded, except in the free text, where no one will be able to count them.

The investigation report on serious or fatal accidents goes further, but it is still built on the same model. Its first part describes the pilot in detail: identity, occupation, age, weight, family situation, qualifications, years of flying, flying frequency, equipment, supervision. The site comes down to an address, a take-off and a landing. The second part leaves the pen free: sequence of events, direct causes, contributing factors, recommendations. This is a real opening, and the warning at the top of the report says so clearly: it “is in no way intended to determine faults or liabilities”[^c-ffvl-rapport-enquete]. But these free fields are neither coded nor counted. System-related factors appear only if the investigator thinks of looking for them. And there is no planned follow-up of recommendations: we do not know to whom they are sent, who must respond to them, or whether they have been applied. Finally, the report is for internal use only and its circulation is very limited. What it teaches stays in a drawer.

The FFVL is nevertheless one of the most advanced in the world in this area of collection and analysis. The aim is not to punish the best pupil in the class. It simply says a lot about the work of the rest. I sometimes write these reports. One concerned a fatal accident in a hike-and-fly competition: a collision with an unmarked goods-lift cable. The direct cause fits in one line. The contributing factors I was able to identify almost all relate to the system: a thin cable, with no intermediate pylon and no markings; a cable missing from most of the obstacle maps used by pilots and their routers; no unified European file of these obstacles; no sporting exclusion zone in a valley that is nonetheless full of cables; no common safety radio channel that would have allowed competitors who had passed earlier to warn the others. Fatigue, altitude and evening light come next. Yet none of these threats has a box in the report form. Once entered into the database, this accident will become a collision with an obstacle, perhaps with a “poor observation” box ticked. In other words, a pilot error.

What we need is a form that follows the logic of TEM (see “Myth #2”): alongside the pilot’s errors, boxes for threats from the environment, the site, the equipment, the organisation and information, and a box for the defences that failed or worked. With that, we could finally count what we today only recount. And every recommendation in an investigation report should have a recipient, an expected response and a public record of what was done about it. All this requires a lot of resources, but first of all it requires a change of mindset.

### Lessons learned (RETEX) and accident database

Every institution should therefore set up a reporting and lessons-learned system (RETEX, *retour d’expérience*). The community lacks a strictly public, free and open-access digital platform. Pilots, clubs, schools and professionals could report an accident and its circumstances, including when they are not directly involved (as witnesses). We must relieve our victims of guilt so that they agree to give an accurate account of what happened to them.

The FFVL has already tried. Around 2012, it opened a “BDNA” on its website, a database for reporting “non-accidents”. Four years later, it acknowledged that it “poorly meets the need for lessons learned” and replaced it with an incident report form. The same document admitted that “in the absence of reliable data on pilots’ flying hours”, data on ratings and accidents were “difficult to interpret”[^c-ffvl-bdna]. The intention was good. What was missing was what keeps a lessons-learned system alive: the trust of those who report, the publication of what is learned, and activity data to relate accidents to actual flying.

At international level, we could act by harmonising data. Offer a database to those who have none. Map accidents by severity, classify them by “similarity”, and try to tackle with TEM (Threat and Error Management) methods the two or three largest families of threats or “pilot errors” of the moment.

### Fatality List

Tribute, empathy, warning. A list of pilots and passengers who have died in free flight would be welcome and would change the way we see our activity. BASE jumping has such a public list of names. It counted just over 500 people mourned in 2025. Who knows how many a paragliding or hang gliding list would count? We need to reset our perceived risk by looking at what we take part in from a new angle. We must take up Rick Masters’ work and make it reliable (see “Who counts our dead?”). There are already, without any doubt, several thousand free-flying pilots who have died. Let us own our impact on society and reflect a more realistic image of what free flight still is today.

### A victim support association

We must help victims and their families more effectively and with more empathy. Each federation could set up a fund, with contributions in proportion to its membership, paid into an international non-profit organisation. This would make it possible to promote and provide emergency support to free-flight victims. Psychological, financial and legal support, search operations, organising rescue, repatriation, support for families, home adaptations, disability and death insurance advice. There is so much to do!
